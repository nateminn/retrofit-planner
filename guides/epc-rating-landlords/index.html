EPC Rating for Landlords: Rules, Costs, and How to Improve

The short answer

  • Band E is the minimum to let a home today, and fines for breaking the rules reach £5,000
  • Band C for every privately rented home from 1 October 2030, with fines of up to £30,000 and a £10,000 spending cap
  • About £1,200 for loft insulation, heating controls and LED lighting, often enough to move from F to E
Current rulesCostsReach band EBand C by 2030ExemptionsGrantsFAQs

Landlords in England and Wales must have a minimum EPC rating of E to legally let a property. Fines for non-compliance reach £5,000. The government has confirmed that from 1 October 2030 every privately rented home must meet a band C equivalent standard, with fines of up to £30,000 per property per breach. In England 48% of privately rented homes were already in band C in 2024 (English Housing Survey). This guide covers the current rules, what happens if your property fails, the cheapest upgrades to improve your rating, available exemptions, and how to prepare for stricter requirements. To check your own property, use the landlord EPC calculator.

Current rules: what landlords must do

Band E is the legal minimum to let. Properties rated F or G cannot be let unless an exemption is registered, and fines reach a maximum of £5,000 per property.

How far homes of each age are from band C: EPC rating by house age.

Read the detail

The Minimum Energy Efficiency Standards (MEES) regulations, in force since April 2018 for new tenancies and April 2020 for all tenancies, set the rules. Here is what they require.

Minimum rating: Band E. Properties rated F or G cannot be let unless an exemption is registered.

Scope: All privately rented domestic properties in England and Wales with a valid EPC. This includes houses, flats, and HMOs.

When you need an EPC: When marketing a property to let, and to give a copy to a new tenant. An EPC is valid for 10 years. You do not need a new one for tenancy renewals if the existing certificate is still valid.

Enforcement: Local authorities enforce MEES. Letting a substandard property without a valid exemption can bring a fine of up to £2,000 if the breach lasts under three months and up to £4,000 if it lasts three months or more. False information on the exemptions register can cost up to £1,000, and ignoring a compliance notice up to £2,000. The maximum is £5,000 in total per property. Marketing a home without an EPC at all is a separate £200 penalty under the EPC rules.

Check your property's current rating on the GOV.UK EPC register.

Enforcement is getting tougher

Local authorities can check the public EPC register and the PRS Exemptions Register, and the 2030 rules raise the maximum fine to £30,000 per property per breach. Do not assume you will not be caught.

How much does it cost to improve your EPC?

About £1,200 is often enough to move from F to E. That covers loft insulation, heating controls and LED lighting; reaching C usually needs wall insulation or a heating upgrade, with spending capped at £10,000 under the 2030 rules.

The most cost-effective approach is to start with the cheapest measures per point. Loft insulation, heating controls and LED lighting alone can move many properties from F to E for about £1,200 at typical prices, and cavity wall insulation adds about 8 more points where the walls allow.

Cheapest upgrades to reach Band E

Start with loft insulation, then cavity walls, heating controls, LEDs and draught-proofing. An F home needs between 1 and 18 more points to reach E at 39, and a bare loft costs about £750 to insulate.

Read the detail

If your property is rated F (21 to 38 points), you need between 1 and 18 more points to reach E at 39. Here is the most cost-effective path:

Step 1: Loft insulation. If the property has less than 270mm, top it up. It is one of the best returns per pound spent. A landlord can install loft insulation during a void period for a few hundred pounds in materials, pay about £750 to have 270mm laid in a bare loft (Energy Saving Trust), or get it free through ECO4 if the tenant qualifies.

Step 2: Cavity wall insulation. If the property has unfilled cavity walls (most properties built 1930 to 1990), professional injection costs about £2,200 for a typical home (Energy Saving Trust). Homes that had it fitted used a median 12.0% less gas afterwards (DESNZ NEED, 2026). Read Is Cavity Wall Insulation Worth It? for the full analysis.

Step 3: Heating controls. Smart heating controls cost £150 to £400 and add about 3 points in our model. Where there are no controls at all, a programmer, room thermostat and thermostatic radiator valves (TRVs) cost around £600 (Energy Saving Trust).

Step 4: Lighting. Replacing all halogen and incandescent bulbs with LEDs costs £100 to £300 for a whole house and adds 2 or 3 points in our model. This is an easy win.

Step 5: Draught-proofing. Sealing gaps around windows, doors, and letterboxes costs about £250 for a whole house done professionally (Energy Saving Trust). Draught excluder strips are a cheap DIY option.

Use the EPC recommendations

Every EPC certificate lists specific recommended improvements for that property, with estimated costs and savings. Start there. Use our EPC improvement planner to model the impact of different upgrade combinations before spending money.

Preparing for a minimum of C

Every privately rented home must meet a band C equivalent standard by 1 October 2030. Landlords must spend up to £10,000 per property including VAT, and the maximum fine rises to £30,000 per property per breach.

Read the detail

In its response of 21 January 2026, the government confirmed that every privately rented home in England and Wales must meet a band C equivalent standard by 1 October 2030, with no earlier date for new tenancies. Until then the band E minimum still applies. Homes will be judged on the new EPC metrics: first a primary standard on fabric performance, then a secondary standard on either the heating system or smart readiness, whichever the landlord chooses. A home rated C or above on the current rating, on an EPC lodged before 1 October 2029, counts as compliant until that certificate expires or is replaced.

Landlords will have to spend up to £10,000 per property, including VAT, and homes worth less than £100,000 have a lower cap of 10% of their value. If the property still falls short at the cap, the cost cap exemption lasts 10 years. The maximum fine rises to £30,000 per property per breach. Landlords who plan ahead will spend less overall than those who do the minimum now and face a second round of upgrades later.

Moving from E to C can cost anything from a few hundred pounds to the full £10,000 cap, depending on the walls and heating. Common routes include:

For properties with solid walls: solid wall insulation is often the only way to reach C. Internal insulation costs about £12,000 for a typical 3 bed semi and external about £15,000 (Energy Saving Trust), so in many solid walled homes the £10,000 cap will apply.

For properties with gas boilers: a new condensing boiler (about £3,500 for a 3 bed home, the DESNZ average) combined with insulation can reach C. A heat pump would push the rating higher, about 12 points against an old gas boiler in our model against about 8 for a new boiler, but it costs more: a median £12,700 for a 3 bed semi, or about £5,200 after the £7,500 grant. Check costs with our heat pump calculator.

For properties already at D: adding solar panels (£5,000 to £7,000 for a 4 kW system, about 8 points in our model) can lift the current rating to C where the roof suits, and a 4 kW system pays for itself in about 11 to 12 years on a south facing roof. Under the 2030 rules fabric comes first, so check how solar will count against the standard your home is judged on before relying on it.

Exemptions from MEES

Register an exemption if E is out of reach. Exemptions last 5 years; the cost cap exemption applies when the remaining improvements would cost more than £3,500 including VAT, rising to £10,000 under the 2030 rules.

Read the detail

If your property genuinely cannot reach E, or if improvements are disproportionately expensive, you may register an exemption. Exemptions must be registered on the PRS Exemptions Register and are valid for 5 years.

Cost cap exemption: If all relevant improvements on the EPC recommendations list have been made, or if the remaining improvements would cost more than £3,500 (including VAT), you can register this exemption. Under the 2030 rules the cap rises to £10,000 and this exemption will last 10 years.

Wall insulation exemption: If a surveyor confirms that cavity wall or solid wall insulation is not suitable or would damage the property (for example, due to damp risk or structural issues), you can claim this exemption.

Consent exemption: If the tenant refuses to allow improvements, or if a third party (such as a planning authority or freeholder) refuses necessary consent, this exemption applies.

Devaluation exemption: If an independent surveyor confirms that specific improvements would reduce the property's market value by more than 5%, you can register this exemption. This is rare but relevant for properties where external insulation would alter the character of a period building.

Important: exemptions do not pass to a new owner. If a let property is sold, the exemption stops applying and the new owner must improve the property or register their own exemption. When an exemption expires, you must either improve the property or register a new exemption with updated evidence. A temporary exemption is also available to people who have recently become a landlord.

Grants available to landlords

Some, but fewer than owner-occupiers get. The £7,500 Boiler Upgrade Scheme grant applies when replacing fossil fuel heating, 0% VAT applies to rental homes, and ECO4 can help if the tenant gets a qualifying benefit.

Read the detail

Landlords have access to fewer grant programmes than owner-occupiers, but some funding is available.

ECO4: Some ECO4 measures are available for privately rented properties where the tenant receives a qualifying benefit. The scheme ends on 31 December 2026. Read the full ECO4 guide.

0% VAT: Insulation, solar panels, and heat pumps installed in residential properties qualify for 0% VAT until 31 March 2027. This applies to rental properties as well as owner-occupied homes.

BUS grant: The Boiler Upgrade Scheme grant of £7,500 for a heat pump, or £9,000 until March 2027 where the home is heated by oil or LPG with no mains gas connection, is available for rental properties in England and Wales, provided the landlord is replacing an existing fossil fuel heating system. Since 28 April 2026 the scheme no longer asks for a valid EPC.

Tax treatment of EPC improvements

Improvement costs can be offset for tax. Like-for-like replacements such as a new boiler are allowable expenses in the year, while new capital improvements such as insulation or solar count against Capital Gains Tax on sale.

Read the detail

Landlords can offset the cost of energy efficiency improvements against rental income for tax purposes. Replacement like-for-like items (such as a new boiler replacing an old one) are allowable expenses in the year they are incurred. Capital improvements (such as adding insulation where none existed or installing solar panels) are treated as capital expenditure and can be offset against Capital Gains Tax when the property is sold.

Speak to your accountant about the most tax-efficient approach to funding improvements. In many cases, phasing improvements over two or more tax years can be more efficient than doing everything at once.

EPC ratings and property value

A better rating helps letting because tenants notice bills. The median band E home costs about £479 a year more to run than the median band C home at October 2026 prices.

Read the detail

Tenants are increasingly aware of energy costs when choosing a rental property, helped by the rule that the EPC rating must appear in property adverts. On government meter data, the median band E home costs about £479 a year more to run than the median band C home at October 2026 prices (see energy bills by EPC rating), which is the number tenants will increasingly ask about.

For landlords considering the investment, any combination of easier letting, fewer void periods and a better rating on the listing can shorten the effective payback period of energy improvements beyond what pure bill savings suggest.

Getting a new EPC

£55 to £150, and usually under an hour. A typical two or three bedroom house costs £65 to £95; give the assessor loft access and invoices for any improvements to get the best rating.

Order an EPC, gas safety certificate and PAT test together from Energy Performance Certificates, a national provider, or compare local assessors on the government register of assessors.

Read the detail

An EPC assessment costs £55 to £150, and £65 to £95 for a typical two or three bedroom house, and takes under an hour for most homes (see how much an EPC costs). The assessor inspects the property's construction, insulation, heating system, glazing, and lighting. They enter the data into SAP (Standard Assessment Procedure) software, which generates the rating.

Tips for getting the best possible rating from your assessment: ensure the assessor can access the loft to verify insulation depth, provide documentation for any improvements (invoices, certificates), make sure all rooms are accessible, and point out features like cavity wall insulation, double glazing, and heating controls that may not be obvious.

Frequently asked questions

What is the minimum EPC rating for landlords in 2026?

The current minimum is E. Properties rated F or G cannot be let unless an exemption is registered, and fines reach £5,000. From 1 October 2030 every privately rented home must meet a band C equivalent standard, with fines of up to £30,000 per property per breach. Use our EPC improvement planner to see how to reach E at the lowest cost.

How much does it cost to improve an EPC from F to E?

It depends on the home. Loft insulation (about £750 in a bare loft), heating controls (£150 to £400) and LED lighting (£100 to £300) add about 12 to 13 points in our model for about £1,200, which is often enough. Add cavity wall insulation (about £2,200) if the walls allow.

Do I need a new EPC for every tenancy?

No. An EPC is valid for 10 years. You need one when marketing and when a new tenancy begins. Check your current rating on the GOV.UK EPC register.

What happens if my rental property has an F or G rating?

You cannot legally let it without a valid exemption. Fines are up to £5,000 per property. Check our grant eligibility tool for funding that could cover improvement costs.

Are there exemptions from the minimum EPC rules?

Yes. The high cost exemption applies when the remaining improvements would cost more than £3,500 including VAT. Others cover unsuitable wall insulation, tenant or third party refusal, property devaluation of more than 5%, and people who have recently become a landlord. Exemptions must be registered and most last 5 years. Under the 2030 rules the cost cap rises to £10,000 and that exemption lasts 10 years.
