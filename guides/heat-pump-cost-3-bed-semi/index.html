Heat Pump Cost for a 3-Bed Semi-Detached House: Full 2026 Breakdown

An air source heat pump for a 3-bed semi costs £9,000 to £12,000 installed. The £7,500 Boiler Upgrade Scheme grant brings that down to £1,500 to £4,500, and if you are replacing an oil or LPG boiler the £9,000 grant can cover the heat pump entirely. Add radiator upgrades and a hot water cylinder and the realistic total is £4,300 to £10,000 after the grant. At the Ofgem price cap for October to December 2026 the running cost is £745 a year on a heat pump tariff, £318 less than gas. The 3-bed semi is the most common house in Britain and the sweet spot for heat pump economics, and this guide covers every cost that applies to one.

What a 3-bed semi heat pump really costs

Across every house type in England and Wales, the median air source heat pump installation funded by the Boiler Upgrade Scheme cost £12,908 in April to June 2026, with a quarter of jobs under £11,128 and a quarter over £15,627, according to the Microgeneration Certification Scheme (MCS). A 3-bed semi sits at or below the lower quartile. It needs a smaller unit than a detached house, has fewer radiators to check, shares a wall that cuts heat loss, and usually fits a cylinder in the existing airing cupboard. That is why the realistic range for a semi is £9,000 to £12,000 before the grant.

What size heat pump for a 3-bed semi

Most 3-bed semis end up with an 8 to 10 kW air source heat pump. A post-1990 semi, or an older one that has had its loft topped up to 270mm, its cavity filled and its windows replaced, can manage with 6 to 8 kW and will be cheaper to buy and to run. Semis with solid walls, single glazing or a large extension push towards 10 to 12 kW.

The number that matters is not the bedroom count but the heat loss calculation. Your MCS installer must survey every room and work out how much heat it loses on the design day, around minus 3C in the south of England and colder further north. A typical 90 square metre semi with reasonable insulation loses 4 to 6 kW at that temperature, and the unit is sized to cover that plus hot water with a small margin. If a quote arrives without a room-by-room calculation attached, it was sized by guesswork, and an oversized heat pump costs more, cycles on and off, and runs less efficiently than one matched to the house.

Insulate first to save £1,000 to £3,000

Loft insulation (£300 to £600) and cavity wall insulation (£500 to £900 for a semi) can drop the heat pump you need by 2 kW, which is one size band and roughly £1,000 to £3,000 off the installation. If you receive a qualifying benefit, ECO4 may pay for the insulation outright.

Which heat pump is best for a semi-detached house? The honest answer is that at this size the brands are close, and the design decides the result. Ask for a design flow temperature of 50C or lower, a seasonal coefficient of performance (SCOP) above 3.5 in writing, and an MCS 020 noise assessment for the outdoor unit position. R290 units such as the aroTHERM plus and the Cosy range run hotter, which can save a radiator or two in a 1930s semi. The Ecodan and Altherma ranges have the widest parts availability if you plan to use a local independent installer. Our heat pump noise guide explains the noise figures.

Radiators, cylinder and pipework

A heat pump heats water to 40 to 50C instead of the 65 to 75C a gas boiler uses, so each radiator has to be larger to deliver the same warmth. A 3-bed semi typically has 8 to 10 radiators, and 3 to 5 of them need upsizing, usually the living room, the hall and the largest bedrooms. Budget £2,000 to £4,000. Our radiator sizing guide explains how installers decide which ones change and why a double-panel radiator in the same slot often solves it.

You also need a hot water cylinder, normally 170 to 210 litres for a family of three or four. If you have a combi boiler there is no cylinder, so allow £800 to £1,500 for one plus a cupboard to put it in. Most semis have an airing cupboard on the landing that fits a 200 litre cylinder, which is why semis rarely pay the loft-cylinder premium that flats and small terraces do. If you already have a system boiler with a cylinder, it usually still needs replacing, because heat pump cylinders have a larger coil to work at lower temperatures.

The common budget breaker is microbore pipework, the 8 mm or 10 mm pipes fitted in many semis from the 1970s onwards. It restricts flow at heat pump temperatures and often needs replacing with 15 mm or 22 mm pipe at £500 to £1,000. Ask the installer to check pipe sizes during the survey rather than discovering the problem on installation day.

The Boiler Upgrade Scheme for a semi

The Boiler Upgrade Scheme pays £7,500 towards an air source or ground source heat pump in England and Wales, and since July 2026 £9,000 for homes replacing an oil or LPG boiler. For a semi that is the difference between a heat pump that costs £1,500 to £4,500 and one that costs nothing at the lower end of the price range. Your MCS-certified installer applies for the grant and it comes off the invoice. The scheme was extended to 2030 in April 2026, installations carry 0% VAT until March 2027, and a semi qualifies exactly as any other house does provided it has a valid EPC and the heat pump replaces a fossil fuel system. Read our BUS grant guide for the eligibility rules and our grant checker for anything else you might qualify for.

Is a heat pump worth it for a 3-bed semi

Against a new gas boiler at £2,000 to £3,500, a heat pump costs £1,800 to £6,500 more once radiators and the cylinder are included. At £318 a year saving on a heat pump tariff, that is a payback of 6 to 20 years, so the case is strongest when your radiators need little work or your boiler is due for replacement anyway. Against an oil boiler the saving is around £500 a year and the £9,000 grant usually covers the heat pump, so payback is immediate to 8 years. A heat pump also lasts 20 to 25 years compared with 12 to 15 for a boiler. Run the numbers for your own house with our boiler vs heat pump comparison, and read heat pump vs new boiler for the 15-year view.

Ground source is rarely worth considering for a semi. It needs a 100 metre borehole or around 200 square metres of garden, costs £20,000 to £30,000 installed, and saves only £160 a year more than air source on a heat pump tariff. Keep it for large detached plots.

Noise, placement and the party wall

The one cost that hits semis harder than detached houses is placement. The outdoor unit must meet the MCS 020 noise standard, which works out at 42 dB at your neighbour's nearest window, and on a semi that window is often a few metres away. Installers handle it by siting the unit on the far side of the house from the party wall, on the rear wall facing the garden, or on a short stand-off bracket with anti-vibration feet. Modern units are quiet, typically 50 to 55 dB at one metre, so most semis pass without extra work, but a unit forced to sit on the party-wall side may need an acoustic enclosure at £300 to £800.

Planning permission is not normally needed. Air source heat pumps on houses in England are permitted development if the noise assessment passes, the unit is under 1.5 cubic metres and the house is not listed or in a conservation area facing a road. Our planning permission guide covers the exceptions. It is worth telling your neighbour before the survey rather than after the crane arrives.

Getting quotes: what to check

Get three quotes from MCS-certified installers, and compare them on the design rather than the headline price. Each quote should include:

  • A room-by-room heat loss calculation with the design outdoor temperature stated.
  • The design flow temperature, ideally 50C or lower, and the SCOP the installer expects at that temperature.
  • A radiator schedule listing all 8 to 10 emitters, which ones change, and their new sizes.
  • The cylinder size and where it will go, ideally the existing airing cupboard.
  • An MCS 020 noise assessment for the outdoor unit position, with the neighbour's nearest window marked.
  • The warranty on the unit, usually 5 to 7 years, the warranty on the installation, and who you call when something fails.
  • Confirmation that the installer will apply for the Boiler Upgrade Scheme and deduct it from the invoice.

Installation takes 2 to 3 days for a semi, and up to 4 when most radiators are being swapped. Find installers through the MCS installer search, and read our old house guide if your semi has solid walls.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a heat pump cost for a 3-bed semi-detached house?

£9,000 to £12,000 installed. After the £7,500 BUS grant you pay £1,500 to £4,500 for the heat pump, or £4,300 to £10,000 with radiators and a cylinder.

What size heat pump does a 3-bed semi need?

8 to 10 kW for most semis, 6 to 8 kW for a well-insulated post-1990 one. The installer's heat loss calculation decides. Insulate first to bring the size and cost down.

How much does a heat pump cost to run in a 3-bed semi?

£745 a year on a heat pump tariff, £1,089 on standard electricity, against £1,063 for gas at the October to December 2026 price cap. See our running costs guide.

Which heat pump is best for a semi-detached house?

Any 8 to 10 kW unit from Vaillant, Mitsubishi, Daikin, Samsung or Octopus will heat a 3-bed semi well. Choose on the design, the flow temperature and the SCOP in the quote, and on where the outdoor unit can sit relative to next door's windows.

Is a heat pump worth it for a 3-bed semi?

Yes on a heat pump tariff or off the gas grid. You save £318 a year against gas and around £500 against oil, and the unit lasts 20 to 25 years. Against a cheap boiler swap on a standard tariff the saving disappears. Compare with our boiler vs heat pump tool.

Do I need planning permission for a heat pump on a semi?

Usually not. It is permitted development in England if the noise assessment passes and the house is not listed or in a conservation area facing a road. See our planning permission guide.
