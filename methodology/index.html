Our Methodology

Figures last reviewed 25 September 2026. Energy prices are the Ofgem cap for 1 October to 31 December 2026 and change quarterly; the next cap is announced in late November.

Every number on Retrofit Planner comes from a published, verifiable source. Here's exactly how our calculators work, what data they use, and where we make assumptions.

Energy prices

We use Ofgem's quarterly price cap announcements as the basis for all energy cost calculations. Our current rates are from the price cap for 1 October to 31 December 2026, announced on 26 August 2026:

  • Electricity: 26.32p per kWh, standing charge 54.83p per day
  • Gas: 7.97p per kWh, standing charge 29.68p per day
  • Heating oil: 9.0p per kWh, about 93p a litre. Kerosene cost more in September 2026 (BoilerJuice put the UK average at 117p a litre on 24 September, roughly 11p per kWh), so our oil figures understate what an oil heated home spends and would save by switching.
  • LPG: 9.5p per kWh (typical bulk contract)
  • Heat pump tariff: 18p per kWh effective, assuming a well-scheduled time-of-use tariff such as Octopus Cosy with around 60% of heating shifted into the cheap windows

We update our calculators when Ofgem announces each new price cap (usually late February, May, August and November), so the figures cover the coming quarter. We do not account for future price changes in our calculations because these are unpredictable. All estimates reflect current prices only.

Installation costs

Heat pumps: the median cost installers reported for air source heat pumps paid under the Boiler Upgrade Scheme in 2025/26, by size (DESNZ Boiler Upgrade Scheme statistics, August 2026 release, Table A1.3A, 30,590 installations). Installers report it on the MCS certificate, and it covers the system itself, labour and VAT, before the grant is taken off. We read it at the size our model gives the home, interpolating between size bands. Where we show a range it is the scheme's own lower and upper quartile for April to June 2026 (Table Q1.1A), scaled to that median. Some homes also need larger radiators, which can add to the cost.

Insulation: the Energy Saving Trust's 2026 figures for a typical home: £750 to lay 270mm of loft insulation in a bare loft and £600 to top one up, about £2,700 for cavity wall insulation, and about £12,000 internal or £15,000 external for solid wall insulation on a typical 3 bed semi. Larger homes cost more.

Boilers: our own assumption of £1,500 to £3,500 for a new condensing gas boiler, scaled by home size. There is no official median for boiler replacement, and installers' prices vary widely.

Heat pump calculations

  • Heat demand: from the DESNZ National Energy Efficiency Data-Framework (NEED) 2026, covering 2024 consumption in England and Wales. We took median gas consumption for every property type and floor area band from the 39,502 gas heated records in the anonymised sample, fitted it against floor area for each type, then scaled it by bedroom count so the population weighted result reproduces NEED's own published medians by bedrooms (5,215 kWh for 1 bed up to 19,339 kWh for 5 or more). It matches to within 0.3% for 2 to 5 bedrooms. Every calculator that compares heating costs, and the embeddable widget, loads this one table from /js/heat-model.js. Guide pages state figures derived from it, and checks in the repository compare those figures against the table so they cannot drift unnoticed.
  • Insulation adjustment: measured from the same records by comparing EPC bands within each property type and floor area cell, so that size is not counted twice. Across 17 such cells the medians relative to band D are A or B 0.62, C 0.85, D 1.00, E 1.11.
  • Efficiency: the whole system seasonal performance measured in 428 air source heat pumps by the government's Electrification of Heat trial (final report, December 2024): median 2.78, with the middle half of homes between 2.55 and 3.05. We use 2.55 for a poorly insulated home, 2.78 for average, 3.05 for well insulated and 3.25 for a recently retrofitted one, our estimate of about the top tenth, since the report gives only the median and quartiles. Until 24 September 2026 we used 2.6 to 3.4, which was a little optimistic; see the comparison.
  • Size: the home's annual heat demand over 1,100 full load hours, which gives the sizes installers typically fit: about 9 kW for an average 3 bed semi. The right size for a particular home comes from the installer's room by room heat loss calculation.
  • Grant: the Boiler Upgrade Scheme pays £7,500 towards an air source heat pump, or £9,000 for homes replacing oil or LPG until March 2027.
  • Running costs: heat demand divided by efficiency, multiplied by the electricity rate, compared with heat demand divided by the existing boiler's seasonal efficiency (90% for gas, 85% for oil and LPG, 100% for electric heating) multiplied by that fuel's rate. Both are energy only. Gas also carries a £108 a year standing charge, which a household saves if it caps the gas supply after switching; the calculators show that separately.
  • Your own bill: if you enter your bill, we take off the gas standing charge, work out the heat your home actually uses from the rest, and cost the heat pump on that same demand, so both sides describe the same house.

Boiler or heat pump, and when we offer quotes

  • Boiler vs heat pump: the 15 year total is the installed cost plus 15 years of energy plus servicing of £100 a year for either system, at today's prices with no discounting. The heat pump is shown on the standard electricity rate and on an 18p heat pump tariff. The gas standing charge of about £108 a year is left out of both, because you only save it if you cap the gas supply. The verdict follows the heat pump tariff case, which is how most owners run one.
  • Quote offers: the heat pump calculator offers installer quotes only where a heat pump saves more than £60 a year and pays back within 25 years on a heat pump tariff, after the grant. The boiler comparison offers them only where the heat pump is cheaper over 15 years on a heat pump tariff, and the solar calculator only where the panels pay for themselves with years of their life left. The insulation calculator, EPC planner and retrofit plan offer them with every result.

Insulation savings

Every insulation saving is measured, not modelled. The government compares the gas use of homes before and after a measure is fitted against similar homes that had nothing done, and publishes the median: 12.0% for cavity wall insulation across 2,191 homes, 17.3% for solid wall insulation across 2,411, and 3.2% for loft insulation across 1,486, most of them top-ups (NEED Impact of Measures 2026, Table 1). We apply those percentages to the home's own fuel use from the heat model and price them at the fuel it uses. When several measures are combined, each saving is taken from what is left after the ones before it.

Measured savings are lower than the modelled figures most calculators use, because many homes were under-heated before and take part of the gain as warmth rather than a lower bill. A home heated the same way before and after can save more than we show.

Retrofit plan

The retrofit plan combines the two. It lists the insulation a home may need, cheapest payback first, applies each measured saving in turn, then sizes and costs a heat pump for the home as it will be after that insulation. A whole-plan total counts the heat pump only where it lowers the bill. The same script runs the embeddable version, so the two cannot disagree.

Solar panel calculations

Our solar calculator uses:

  • Generation: regional figures of 810 to 950 kWh per kW installed a year, set a little below PVGIS (Photovoltaic Geographical Information System, European Commission Joint Research Centre) city figures to allow for shading and roof pitch. Roof direction scales that output using PVGIS runs for London, Manchester and Edinburgh: south-east 0.95, south-west 0.93, east and west 0.80, a flat roof with tilted frames 0.90.
  • Self-use ratio: read from the MCS MGD 003 Issue 2.0 look-up tables, which set self-consumption from occupancy, annual electricity use and system generation rather than a single figure. A 4 kW array on a medium user who is out half the day self consumes about 19%, rising to about 52% with a 5 kWh battery.
  • Installed cost: bands of £4,000 to £5,500 for 3 kW up to £8,500 to £10,000 for 6 kW, checked against the DESNZ solar PV cost data published 28 May 2026 (median about £1,550 to £1,600 per kW installed). Payback uses the middle of the band.
  • Battery cost: £4,600 for a 5 kWh system, the Energy Saving Trust figure, added to the system cost when a battery is selected. A battery lasts about 10 to 12 years, so the 25 year figures include one replacement.
  • Smart Export Guarantee: we use 12p per kWh, the Outgoing Octopus rate for Octopus electricity customers. Export tariffs open to customers of any supplier paid less, from 4.1p per kWh, and rates checked on 25 September 2026 ran from 4.1p to 17.5p depending on supplier and tariff.
  • Panel degradation: 0.5% per year, which is the industry standard warranty assumption.

EPC calculations

Our EPC improvement planner is a simple points model, not an assessment. It estimates a score from the answers you give using fixed points for each feature, then adds typical points for each improvement. It follows the SAP bands (A 92 to 100 down to G 1 to 20) but does not run SAP, and we have not yet compared its estimates with lodged certificates. Treat the band as a starting point: only an accredited assessor can produce an EPC.

Grant eligibility

Our grant checker uses the published eligibility criteria from GOV.UK and the scheme administrators, and was last reviewed in September 2026. Schemes open, close and change, so always confirm eligibility with the scheme itself before you rely on it.

How we test the model

On 24 September 2026 we tested the model against the largest sets of measured data available for UK homes: installed costs from 30,590 grant funded heat pumps, efficiency from 428 monitored heat pumps, and insulation savings measured in thousands of homes. Where it was off, we changed it. How accurate are our figures? shows what we tested, what we found and what we changed.

Limitations and disclaimers

Our calculators provide estimates based on national averages and published data. They are not substitutes for professional assessments. Key limitations include:

  • Actual costs vary by location, installer, property condition, and access difficulty.
  • Energy prices change quarterly and we cannot predict future prices.
  • EPC improvements depend on the specific property and assessor methodology.
  • Grant eligibility rules change and may vary by local authority area.
  • All calculations run in your browser and we do not store the figures you enter. After you calculate, they are added to the page address so the result can be kept or shared; our privacy policy explains what that means.

Always obtain professional quotes and assessments before proceeding with any installation.
