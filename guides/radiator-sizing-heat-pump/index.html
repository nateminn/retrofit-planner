Radiator Sizing for Heat Pumps: Do You Need to Upgrade?

Most homes switching to a heat pump need some radiator changes, but rarely all of them. A heat pump runs most efficiently when the water it sends round is cool, and a radiator gives out far less heat at those temperatures. The MCS Heat Emitter Guide says that with water at 41 to 45C, a radiator's standard rated output needs to be about 3.1 times the room's heat loss, against 1.9 times at 51 to 55C. This guide explains how radiator sizing works with heat pumps, how to check whether your radiators are big enough, and when underfloor heating is worth considering instead.

Why heat pumps need different radiators

Radiators in homes built around a gas boiler were usually sized to run hot. The bigger the gap between the water temperature and the room, the faster a radiator gives out heat, which is why a small radiator can heat a large room from a boiler.

A heat pump works the other way: the cooler the water it has to make, the more heat it gets from each unit of electricity. In the Electrification of Heat trial for DESNZ, the median heat pump ran at 3.50 while producing water at 35C, 2.38 at 55C and 2.02 above 65C. Running at 55C instead of 35C used about 47% more electricity for the same heat.

The solution is not to run the heat pump hotter. It is to install radiators with enough surface area to heat each room at the lower flow temperature. Read our running costs guide for how flow temperature affects your annual bills.

What radiator changes cost depends on how many rooms fall short and what goes in their place, so ask for them to be itemised in each quote; see our heat pump cost by house type guide for the heat pump itself. The £7,500 Boiler Upgrade Scheme grant in England and Wales comes off the installer's price for the whole job. Reducing heat loss through insulation cuts the number of radiators that need changing.

How to check your existing radiators

Your MCS installer works out each room's heat loss and compares it with what each radiator gives at the planned flow temperature. This is part of a proper heat pump design and should be in your quote.

As a rough check, find each radiator's rated output, usually on a label or in the manufacturer's data, and divide it by the factor in the table for your planned flow temperature. Older radiators are sometimes rated at a 60C difference; MCS converts those to the standard rating by multiplying by 0.825. At 45C, a radiator rated at 1,000 watts gives about 320 watts.

If 320 watts covers the room's heat loss on a cold day, the radiator can stay. If the room needs 700 watts, it needs replacing with a larger one or supplementing.

Which radiators usually need replacing

Small single-panel radiators in large rooms. These are the most common candidates. A radiator rated at 800 watts gives only about 260 watts with water at 45C.

Bathrooms with towel rails. Towel radiators have limited surface area and can struggle at heat pump temperatures. You may need a larger towel radiator or an extra panel radiator.

Kitchens and hallways with small radiators. These rooms often have the smallest radiators because space was limited when the system was originally designed. They frequently need upgrading.

Which radiators often survive

Large cast iron radiators in period homes. Big old radiators can have a lot of surface area for the room they heat, so check their output before replacing them. If your old house has original large radiators, they may be an asset rather than a liability.

Oversized modern radiators. Some plumbers historically fitted radiators larger than strictly necessary. If your existing radiators are double-panel convectors (the thick type with fins between two panels), they may have enough output at lower temperatures.

Rooms in well-insulated parts of the house. If a room has new double glazing, insulated walls, and minimal draughts, its heat demand is low enough that even a modest radiator may cope. This is another reason to insulate before installing a heat pump.

Insulate first to reduce radiator upgrades

Better insulation cuts each room's heat loss, so existing radiators are more likely to be big enough. In the MCS Heat Emitter Guide's worked example, cavity wall insulation, double glazing, underfloor insulation and draught-proofing cut a room's heat loss from 1,671 W to 976 W; with a double convector radiator of the same size, the room went from needing water above 60C to working at 45C. Start with loft insulation, cavity wall insulation and draught-proofing.

Replacement options

Larger panel radiators

The most common solution is a radiator with more output in the same space. In the MCS worked example, swapping a 1,600 by 700 mm double panel radiator for a double convector with the same face size raised its rated output from 1,938 W to 3,269 W, about 70% more, often without moving the pipes.

Fan-assisted radiators

These have a built-in fan that blows air across the heat exchanger, which raises output at low flow temperatures. In the MCS guide, a fan assisted radiator needs to be rated at 2.4 times the room's heat loss at 41 to 45C against 3.1 for an ordinary radiator, so it can be about a quarter smaller, or about a third smaller at 35C. They suit rooms where there is no space for a larger radiator.

The fan uses some electricity and makes some noise, so check the specification before fitting one in a bedroom.

Underfloor heating

Underfloor heating (UFH) can run at the lowest flow temperatures, where a heat pump is most efficient, and the MCS guide covers underfloor systems at 35C and below. It also spreads heat evenly across the room.

However, retrofitting UFH means lifting the existing floor and usually raises floor levels, so it is realistically only practical during a major renovation or when floors are being replaced anyway. For new extensions or ground floor renovations, it is worth specifying from the start.

What your installer should provide

A competent MCS installer will provide a room-by-room heat loss calculation showing the heat demand of each room in watts, the output of each existing radiator at the proposed flow temperature, identification of which radiators need upgrading, and recommended replacements with costs.

This room by room calculation should be part of any professional heat pump quote. If an installer proposes a heat pump without one, that is a red flag. Find certified installers on the MCS directory.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need new radiators for a heat pump?

Most homes need some changes, but rarely every radiator. MCS guidance says that with water at 41 to 45C a radiator's rated output needs to be about 3.1 times the room's heat loss. Your MCS installer works it out room by room. Insulating first reduces the number needing replacement.

How much does it cost to upgrade radiators?

It depends on how many rooms fall short and what goes in their place, so ask for radiator work to be itemised in each quote. See our cost by house type guide for the full picture.

Can I keep my existing radiators?

Sometimes. Large radiators, double convectors and radiators in well-insulated rooms may be big enough already. Your installer's room by room calculation confirms it.

Is underfloor heating better than radiators for a heat pump?

Thermally, yes: it can run at the lowest flow temperatures, where heat pumps are most efficient. Practically, retrofitting it means lifting floors, so it is usually only worth it during a major renovation. Larger radiators are the practical choice for most homes.

What flow temperature should a heat pump run at?

As low as your radiators allow. In the Electrification of Heat trial, the median heat pump ran at 3.50 units of heat per unit of electricity when producing water at 35C, 2.38 at 55C and 2.02 above 65C, so running at 55C rather than 35C used about 47% more electricity for the same heat.
