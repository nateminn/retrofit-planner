Are Solar Panels Worth It in the UK in 2026?

The short answer

  • £5,000 to £7,000 for a typical 4 kW system, at 0% VAT until 31 March 2027
  • About £490 to £560 a year in bill savings and export payments on a south facing roof
  • About 11 to 12 years to pay for itself, or about 21 years with a battery
CostsSavingsPaybackDownsidesBatteriesHeat pumpsFAQs

For most homeowners with a sunny, unshaded roof, yes. A typical 4 kW system costs £5,000 to £7,000, brings in about £490 to £560 a year in bill savings and export payments on a south facing roof, and pays for itself in about 11 to 12 years. Solar installations are zero rated for VAT until 31 March 2027. How well it works for you depends on your roof, where you live and when you are at home. Here is the full picture.

The difference between "with battery" and "without battery" is striking. Without a battery you export around four fifths of your solar generation at 12p per kWh. With a 5 kWh battery you use about 52% yourself at the full 26.32p per kWh rate. But the battery adds only about £180 a year overall, because every kWh you store is one you no longer get paid to export, and it needs replacing after 10 to 12 years. On solar alone it does not pay for itself.

Payback period by scenario

A 4 kW system in southern England with a south facing roof and no battery pays for itself in about 10.7 years: £6,000 divided by £559 a year. The same system pays back in 12.1 years in northern England and 12.3 years in Scotland, and in 13.2 years on an east or west facing roof. Add a 5 kWh battery and payback stretches to about 21 years, because the battery wears out after 10 to 12 years and has to be bought again before the system has paid for itself. After payback, the panels keep earning for the rest of a life of 25 years or more.

Over 25 years, allowing for panels losing 0.5% of their output a year, a 4 kW system without a battery returns about £7,200 more than it cost at today's prices. With a battery, and one replacement battery, the figure falls to about £2,200. Neither figure includes replacing the inverter. Our solar payback guide breaks this down by region, roof and system size.

The honest downsides

High upfront cost. £4,000 to £10,000, depending on size, is a significant sum. Unlike a heat pump, which can get the £7,500 Boiler Upgrade Scheme grant, solar has no grant open to every household, although 0% VAT helps. In England, the Warm Homes: Local Grant can pay for panels for households on lower incomes, usually £36,000 a year or less, in homes rated EPC D to G. The payback is real but takes about 11 to 12 years to arrive on a south facing roof.

Roof dependency. South facing roofs generate the most. East or west facing roofs produce about 20% less, according to PVGIS modelling for a 35 degree roof. North facing roofs are generally not recommended. Your roof also needs to be in good condition, as panels are installed for 25 years or more and removing them for roof repairs is expensive.

Winter shortfall. In London a 4 kW array makes about 142 kWh in December against 477 kWh in July, so December produces about 30% of what July does, and October to March together produce only about a third of the year's output. You will still rely heavily on grid electricity in winter, which is when you use the most.

Inverter replacement. The inverter, which converts the panels' direct current into the alternating current your home uses, needs replacing after around 12 years, according to the Energy Saving Trust. Most come with at least a five year warranty, which can often be extended. Ask installers to price a replacement when they quote, because our 25 year figures leave it out.

Visual impact. Some people dislike the appearance of panels on their roof. Solar panels on houses are usually permitted development, so planning permission is not normally needed, but listed buildings, conservation areas and national parks can have extra rules.

Is a battery worth adding?

The Energy Saving Trust puts home batteries at £1,500 to £10,000, with a 5 kWh system at around £4,600. A battery increases the proportion of solar electricity you use from about 19% to about 52% with a 5 kWh unit, or about 59% with a 10 kWh one, on a 4 kW array. Those are the MCS MGD 003 look-up table figures the solar calculator reads. The extra bill savings are around £330 a year for a 5 kWh battery and around £400 for a 10 kWh one, but you give up export income on the same units, so the net gain is nearer £180 to £220 a year. At £180 a year, a £4,600 battery takes about 26 years to pay back.

The financial case for a battery is strongest if you use most electricity in the evening (when solar is not generating), you are on a time-of-use tariff (charge the battery at cheap rates overnight), or you want maximum independence from the grid.

The financial case is weaker if you work from home and use electricity throughout the day (the MCS tables put self-use at 23% rather than 19%, so there is slightly less daytime surplus for a battery to catch), or the battery cost exceeds the additional savings over its 10 to 12 year lifespan.

You do not have to decide now. A battery can be added to existing panels later, and a retrofitted battery also qualifies for 0% VAT until 31 March 2027. Our solar battery guide works through the numbers.

Why your electricity bill might still be high with solar panels

This is one of the most common complaints from solar panel owners, and it almost always comes down to timing.

Solar panels generate electricity during daylight hours, peaking around midday. If you are at work during the day and use most electricity in the evening (cooking, heating, entertainment), you miss the peak generation window. The solar electricity gets exported at 12p/kWh, and you buy grid electricity at 26.32p/kWh in the evening.

Solutions: add a battery to store daytime generation for evening use, shift high-consumption tasks (dishwasher, washing machine, EV charging) to daytime hours using timers, or switch to a time of use tariff with a cheap overnight rate.

Also check that your system is performing as expected. Shading from trees or new buildings, dirty panels, or an inverter fault can all reduce generation below the forecast.

Solar panels with a heat pump

Solar panels and heat pumps work well together, with a limit worth knowing. Most heat pump demand falls in winter, when panels produce least, so solar covers only part of a heat pump's electricity. It helps most with hot water in summer and with heating in spring and autumn, when the days are longer and some heating is still needed.

A heat pump also raises your electricity use, and the MCS tables show that a bigger household demand means more of your solar is used at home rather than exported, which improves the solar payback. The Energy Saving Trust notes that a battery saves more money running a heat pump, alongside a time of use tariff, than it does storing surplus solar.

If you are planning both improvements, the recommended order is: insulate first, then install the heat pump (claiming the Boiler Upgrade Scheme grant of £7,500, or £9,000 if you are replacing oil or LPG, available until March 2027), then add solar panels. This sequence ensures each improvement is sized correctly for the insulated, heat-pump-heated home.

Effect on EPC rating

In our EPC model, solar panels add about 8 points to your EPC rating. For landlords close to a band boundary, this can be the difference between meeting and missing minimum standards. Combined with insulation and a heat pump, solar helps push a property towards Band B or even A.

Frequently asked questions

Are solar panels worth it in the UK in 2026?

For most homeowners with an unshaded roof facing south, east or west, yes. A 4 kW system costs £5,000 to £7,000, brings in about £490 to £560 a year on a south facing roof and pays for itself in about 11 to 12 years. The 0% VAT rate runs until 31 March 2027.

What is the downside of solar panels in the UK?

The upfront cost (£4,000 to £10,000 depending on size), dependence on roof direction and condition, low winter output (in London December produces about 30% of what July does), an inverter replacement after around 12 years, and the look of panels on your roof. Scotland generates about 15% less than southern England.

Why is my electricity bill still high with solar panels?

Usually because you generate during the day but use most electricity in the evening. Without a battery, a 4 kW array on a home that is empty for about half the day uses only about 19% of its output directly, the MCS MGD 003 figure, and exports the rest at a lower rate. A battery lifts that to about 52% with a 5 kWh unit or about 59% with a 10 kWh one. Running appliances in the daytime on timers also helps.

How long do solar panels take to pay for themselves in the UK?

About 11 to 12 years for a 4 kW system with no battery on a south facing roof: 10.7 years in southern England and 12.3 years in Scotland. With a 5 kWh battery it is about 21 years, because the battery has to be replaced after 10 to 12 years. After payback the panels keep generating for the rest of a life of 25 years or more.

Are solar panels worth it with a battery?

Rarely on solar alone. The Energy Saving Trust prices a 5 kWh battery at around £4,600 and expects it to last 10 to 12 years. On a 4 kW array it lifts self-use from about 19% to about 52%, which adds about £330 a year to bill savings but costs about £150 in lost export income, so the net gain is about £180 a year. The case improves with a time of use tariff, an electric car or a heat pump.

Do solar panels increase house value?

There is no reliable UK figure for how much solar adds to a sale price, so treat any quoted percentage with caution. What a buyer gets is lower electricity bills and a better EPC rating: our EPC model gives solar about 8 points.
