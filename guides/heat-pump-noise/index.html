Heat Pump Noise: How Loud Are They Really?

For planning, what matters is how loud a heat pump is at your neighbour's window. In England, and in Wales since 1 June 2026, a unit installed under permitted development must pass the MCS 020(a) sound calculation: 37 dB(A) or less at 1 metre outside any window or door of a neighbour's habitable room. Every heat pump has a sound power level on its energy label, and the calculation turns that into a level at the window using the distance, nearby walls and any barrier. This guide explains the limit, what affects noise, how to reduce it and what to do if a neighbour is concerned.

What the 37 dB(A) limit means in practice

The table shows how far a heat pump must be from a neighbour's window to pass the test, using the formula in MCS 020(a). The sound power level comes from the unit's energy label; the worked example in the standard uses 60 dB(A).

Open ground counts as one reflecting surface, a wall adds a second and a corner a third. The last column assumes a masonry wall or solid fence at least 18mm thick that completely hides the window from the top of the unit, which the standard counts as 10 dB. Your installer does the real calculation for every neighbouring window.

How a sound is heard depends on more than its level. MCS guidance notes that tonal, intermittent or unfamiliar sounds are more noticeable, that sound at night stands out more against a quieter background, and that people differ widely in how they perceive sound. That is why placement and a conversation with the neighbours matter as much as the number.

Planning rules on noise

In England and Wales, air source heat pumps can be installed under permitted development, without a planning application, if they meet several conditions. The noise condition is the one most people worry about. Scotland and Northern Ireland also require the MCS planning standard or an equivalent.

The 37 dB(A) limit: The MCS 020(a) calculation must give 37.0 dB(A) or less at an assessment position 1 metre outside the centre of any window or door to a habitable room of a neighbouring home. Habitable rooms are rooms used for sleeping or living, not bathrooms, hallways, utility rooms or rooms used only for cooking. If the unit fails, it is not permitted development and you need to apply for planning permission. The older MCS 020 method had a 42 dB(A) limit but counted differently.

How distance helps: Each doubling of distance cuts the level by about 6 dB. A unit with a sound power level of 60 dB(A), on the ground against a wall, gives about 43.0 dB(A) at 4 metres, which fails, and 37.0 dB(A) at 8 metres, which just passes. Put a solid wall between the unit and the window and the 4 metre figure drops to 33.0 dB(A).

Terraced houses and flats: MCS notes that homes in dense streets find the calculation harder to pass, because units sit closer to neighbours' windows. Careful placement, a quieter model or a barrier can bring the result within the limit. Read our planning permission guide for the full rules.

Ask your installer for the noise calculation

An MCS installer must run the MCS 020(a) calculation for every neighbouring window or door that could be affected, keep a copy and give you one. If the result is borderline, they can recommend a quieter unit, a different position or a barrier.

What affects noise levels

Unit size and model

Noise varies from model to model. Every heat pump sold in the UK has to be tested for sound power, and the result, in dB(A), is on its energy label. MCS notes that monobloc units, with all their parts outside, generally make more sound than split systems.

When comparing quotes, ask each installer for the sound power level of the specific model they propose. It is the number the planning calculation starts from, and a quiet mode figure does not count.

Operating mode

A heat pump is more noticeable when it works harder: in winter, when it is heating hot water and during defrost cycles, when it clears ice from the outdoor coil. That is normal, but it is worth planning around, for example by heating hot water outside sleeping hours.

Placement

Where you put the outdoor unit makes a big difference. Hard surfaces within 1 metre of the unit reflect sound and raise the level, and corners and enclosed spaces amplify it. MCS advises facing the unit away from neighbouring windows and your own habitable rooms, and avoiding corners, enclosed areas and hollow spaces, which can amplify noise. More than three reflecting surfaces, as in a lightwell, fails the calculation outright.

Vibration

A heat pump fixed straight to a wall or plinth can pass vibration into the building, which is heard indoors as a low hum separate from the airborne noise. MCS advises against mounting units next to bedrooms or on structures that carry vibration, and recommends anti-vibration feet, pads or rubber grommets.

How to reduce heat pump noise

Choose a quiet unit. Compare sound power levels on the energy labels when you get quotes. A lower figure can be the difference between needing a barrier, or a planning application, and not.

Maximise distance. Each doubling of the distance to your neighbour's window cuts the level by about 6 dB. A position further down the garden helps, though longer pipe runs can add cost.

Use anti-vibration mounts. Rubber feet, pads, platforms or grommets between the heat pump and its plinth or wall stop vibration reaching the building, and MCS notes they may also help the unit last longer.

Avoid reflective surfaces, and use barriers. Do not box the unit in with hard walls. A solid wall or fence at least 18mm thick that hides the neighbour's window from the unit counts as a 10 dB reduction in the calculation, and acoustic fencing can both block and absorb sound. Hedges and gappy fences do not count as barriers.

Ask about a compressor jacket. An insulation jacket round the compressor gives around 6 dB off the compressor's noise, according to MCS, but check with the manufacturer first, because fitting one afterwards may affect the warranty.

Maintain the unit. MCS notes that a lack of maintenance can make a heat pump louder over time. Keep the fan clear of leaves and debris and have loose panels or worn parts fixed.

Use quiet modes and scheduling with care. Quiet or eco modes slow the fan and compressor, but they can also cut heat output and efficiency. Scheduling hot water outside common sleeping times is often the better fix.

Do not restrict airflow to reduce noise

The outdoor unit needs clear airflow around it to work efficiently. Enclosing it too tightly, packing material around the fan, or blocking the air discharge reduces performance and increases running costs. Any noise reduction measure must maintain the manufacturer's minimum clearance distances.

Neighbour concerns

Even when your heat pump meets all planning conditions, a conversation with your neighbours before installation prevents problems later. MCS advises consulting anyone within earshot as early as possible and explaining what they will hear.

Before installation: Let your immediate neighbours know you are installing a heat pump, where the outdoor unit will go and what it will sound like. Showing them the installer's MCS 020(a) result for their window is often reassuring.

If a neighbour complains: MCS suggests checking for a failed or loose component, making sure servicing is up to date, checking whether anything has changed around the unit, such as a removed fence, and then trying quiet modes, scheduling or mitigation such as a barrier.

Statutory nuisance: A neighbour can complain to the council whether or not the unit meets the planning conditions. In England, councils must look into noise that could be a statutory nuisance under the Environmental Protection Act 1990, meaning it unreasonably and substantially interferes with the use or enjoyment of a home, or harms health. If the council finds a nuisance, it must serve an abatement notice.

Indoor noise

The outdoor unit is the main noise source. MCS lists circulating pumps alongside the compressor and fan as the parts that make sound, so you may hear a pump indoors, and a unit fixed without anti-vibration mounts can pass a hum into the building.

Noise and heat pump efficiency

A heat pump is most noticeable when it works hardest, in cold weather, which is also when it is least efficient. In the Electrification of Heat trial, the median heat pump ran at 3.37 units of heat per unit of electricity at 10C outside, but 2.37 at minus 3C. A well-insulated home needs less from the heat pump on the coldest days. This is another reason to insulate your home before or alongside a heat pump installation.

Frequently asked questions

How loud is a heat pump?

It depends on the model and where it sits. Each unit's sound power level is on its energy label, and what matters for planning is the level at your neighbour's window: in England, and in Wales since 1 June 2026, that must be 37 dB(A) or less under the MCS 020(a) calculation for the unit to be permitted development. See our cost by house type guide for the sizes homes need.

What is the planning noise limit for heat pumps?

37 dB(A) at 1 metre outside any window or door of a neighbour's habitable room, worked out with MCS 020(a), in England and in Wales since 1 June 2026. The older MCS 020 method has a 42 dB(A) limit. Read our planning permission guide for all the conditions.

Can neighbours complain about heat pump noise?

Yes. Even if a unit meets the planning rules, a neighbour can complain to the council, which must look into noise that could be a statutory nuisance under the Environmental Protection Act 1990 and serve an abatement notice if it finds one. Good placement, anti-vibration mounts and maintenance prevent most problems.

How can I make my heat pump quieter?

Choose a unit with a lower sound power level, keep it away from corners and hard surfaces, face it away from neighbours' windows, use anti-vibration mounts and a solid barrier where it helps, run hot water outside sleeping hours, and keep the unit maintained.

Are heat pumps noisier in cold weather?

Yes. MCS guidance notes that a heat pump is more noticeable when it works harder in winter, when it heats hot water and during defrost cycles. Better insulation reduces the load.
