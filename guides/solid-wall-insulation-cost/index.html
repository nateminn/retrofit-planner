Solid Wall Insulation Cost in the UK: 2026 Prices, Savings, and Grants

Solid wall insulation costs about £12,000 fitted inside or about £15,000 fitted outside for a typical 3 bed semi, according to the Energy Saving Trust. It gives the biggest measured saving of any insulation: when the government compared gas use before and after in 2,411 homes, against similar homes that had nothing done, the median home used 17.3% less gas after solid wall insulation. For a 3 bed semi heated by gas that is about £151 a year at October 2026 prices, so at full price the work does not pay for itself on bills alone within 50 years. It makes sense when a grant pays for most of it, when you are renovating anyway, or when comfort and damp matter as much as the bill. This guide covers costs, measured savings, grants, damp risks and the choice between internal and external insulation.

How much does solid wall insulation cost?

Internal solid wall insulation costs

Internal wall insulation (IWI) involves fixing insulated plasterboard or a stud frame with mineral wool to the inside face of external walls. It is the cheaper option, needs no scaffolding and does not usually need planning permission, although a listed building needs consent for internal work too.

TypeTypical cost, 3 bed semiWhat is involved
Internal wall insulationAbout £12,000Insulated boards or a stud wall on the inside of every external wall, then replastering and redecorating
External wall insulationAbout £15,000Boards fixed to the outside with scaffolding up, then render or cladding, with sills, pipes and fittings moved out

Source: Energy Saving Trust, updated 18 June 2026. The cost rises with the area of external wall: a mid-terrace has two external walls and a detached house four, so expect a terrace to cost less and a detached house more. Fitting internal insulation when you are redecorating anyway keeps the extra cost down.

External solid wall insulation costs

External wall insulation (EWI) wraps the building in a layer of rigid insulation board, covered with a protective render or cladding system. It requires scaffolding and may need planning permission in conservation areas.

The Energy Saving Trust puts external wall insulation at about £15,000 for a typical 3 bed semi. On top of that, any repairs to existing render, brickwork, gutters or the roof have to be done first, and are not included.

Annual savings from solid wall insulation

The Energy Saving Trust estimates that about a third of the heat lost from an uninsulated home goes through the walls, and solid wall insulation gave the largest measured saving of any insulation. Savings depend on your property type and heating fuel.

Saving: the median 17.3% fall in gas use the government measured in 2,411 homes insulated between May 2023 and May 2024 (NEED Impact of Measures, DESNZ, 11 June 2026, Table 1), applied to each home's typical gas use at average insulation and the October to December 2026 cap price of 7.97p per kWh. This is what homes like yours typically saw, not the most you could save: many homes were under-heated before and take part of the gain as warmth.

If you heat with oil (about 9.0p per kWh) or LPG (about 9.5p per kWh), you save more in pounds: about £181 a year for a 3 bed semi on oil. Homes switching to a heat pump after insulating benefit even more, because the reduced heat demand means a smaller, cheaper heat pump and lower electricity bills.

Simple payback is the cost divided by the yearly saving at October 2026 prices. It leaves out future price changes, the better EPC, and the comfort of warmer walls.

The financial case for solid wall insulation is strongest when combined with grant funding, when you are also installing a heat pump (which benefits from reduced heat loss), or when you count the EPC improvement it delivers. For landlords facing minimum EPC requirements, the investment may be necessary regardless of payback.

Check for ECO4 funding first

Before paying full price, check whether you qualify for ECO4 scheme funding, which can cover the entire cost of solid wall insulation. Even if you do not receive qualifying benefits, your council may refer you through ECO4 Flex or fund the work through a Warm Homes: Local Grant. Use our grant eligibility checker to see your options.

ECO4 closes on 31 December 2026. The work has to be finished by then, not just applied for, and installers fill up as the date gets closer. Government has confirmed there will be no successor supplier obligation: from 2027 the money moves to council run schemes under the Warm Homes Plan, which you apply for through your local authority rather than your energy supplier. If you think you qualify, start now. Check what you qualify for.

Internal vs external: which should you choose?

Choose internal if:

You want the cheaper option and are comfortable losing some room space. The boards add thickness to every external wall, which is noticeable in small rooms. It requires moving radiators, re-routing wiring, and extending window sills. You will need to redecorate after installation. The main advantages are lower cost, no change to the building's external appearance, and usually no need for planning permission or scaffolding.

Internal insulation is often the more acceptable option in a conservation area, where changing the outside needs permission. A listed building needs consent for either kind, so talk to your council's planning team first. See our planning permission guide for restrictions that may affect your project.

Choose external if:

You want the best thermal performance with no disruption to room sizes. External insulation wraps the building in a continuous layer, eliminating cold bridges at floor junctions and around window frames that internal insulation cannot reach. It also protects the existing walls from weather, which can extend their life. The render finish gives old houses a fresh, maintained appearance.

External insulation needs scaffolding for the length of the job, may need planning permission, particularly in a conservation area or where it changes the look of the house, and changes the building's appearance permanently. Window and door reveals become deeper, and you will need to extend or replace sills, soil pipes, and external fittings.

Thermal performance comparison

Both types can reach similar U-values (a measure of heat loss through the wall), and both cut the heat lost through an uninsulated solid wall several times over. External insulation has the edge in practice because it wraps the building in a continuous layer, avoiding the cold spots that internal insulation leaves where floors and internal walls meet the external wall.

What about damp?

Damp is the biggest concern homeowners raise about solid wall insulation, and it is a legitimate one. Done badly, internal wall insulation can trap moisture and create condensation problems. Done correctly, it works without issues for decades.

Before installation: Any existing damp problems must be diagnosed and fixed first. Rising damp, penetrating damp from rainwater, damaged pointing, and leaking gutters and downpipes all need addressing before insulation covers the wall. The Energy Saving Trust is clear that wall insulation cannot go on until damp is resolved and the walls are sound.

Internal insulation and moisture: Internal insulation leaves the old wall colder, so moisture can build up inside it where you cannot see it. The installer should choose materials that suit the wall. Traditional solid walls of brick, stone or lime usually need vapour open (breathable) materials that let the wall dry out, while some board systems rely on a sealed vapour control layer. Either way, the design should be checked for condensation risk and every edge, junction and gap sealed.

External insulation and moisture: External insulation carries lower damp risk because it keeps the original wall warm and above the dew point. Moisture can still pass outward through the wall. The render system must be breathable, not impermeable, to allow this.

Ventilation matters: Insulating walls makes the house more airtight, which raises humidity unless ventilation keeps up. The installer should never block air bricks or vents and should say whether you need more, such as extractor fans. At a minimum, keep trickle vents on windows open and make sure extractor fans in kitchens and bathrooms work. In well sealed homes, mechanical ventilation with heat recovery (MVHR) keeps air fresh while recovering much of the heat that opening windows would lose.

Get a damp survey before insulating

If your property shows any signs of damp, staining, or mould, get a damp survey before proceeding with wall insulation. Covering up an existing damp problem makes it worse. Quality matters too: the Energy Saving Trust reports that 98% of homes fitted with external wall insulation under ECO4 and the Great British Insulation Scheme have problems that need fixing, and TrustMark is carrying out free audits for those homes. Use a TrustMark registered installer and insist on a 25 year guarantee.

Grants and funding for solid wall insulation

The high upfront cost of solid wall insulation means grant funding makes a significant difference to the financial case.

ECO4: The Energy Company Obligation scheme funds insulation for households on qualifying benefits including Universal Credit, Pension Credit, and Child Tax Credit. Solid wall insulation is one of the measures it can fund. ECO4 closes on 31 December 2026, and the work has to be finished by then. Read our ECO4 guide for the full eligibility criteria.

ECO4 Flex: If you do not receive qualifying benefits but live in a low-income household or a property with a low EPC rating, your local council can refer you through the ECO4 Flex route. Eligibility varies by council. Contact your council's energy or housing team to ask.

0% VAT: Installing insulation in a home is zero rated for VAT until 31 March 2027, and 5% after that (HMRC VAT Notice 708/6). It applies to both internal and external insulation fitted by an installer.

Local authority grants: Councils run Warm Homes: Local Grants for lower income households, and some offer other funding. These vary by area and change often, so check with your council directly.

How solid wall insulation affects your EPC

Solid wall insulation is one of the most effective ways to improve your EPC rating. For a home on the edge of a band, it can be enough to move it up one.

This matters especially for landlords, who must meet minimum EPC requirements to let their property. It also matters for homeowners planning to install a heat pump in an old house, where better insulation means a smaller, cheaper heat pump.

The installation process

Internal wall insulation

Installers usually work room by room. The process involves removing skirting boards, radiators, and electrical fittings from each wall. Insulated plasterboard (or a stud frame filled with mineral wool) is fixed to the wall surface with adhesive and mechanical fixings. Where the system needs one, a vapour control layer is sealed behind the plasterboard. Walls are plastered, skirting boards refitted, radiators rehung on longer brackets, and electrical sockets extended. You will need to redecorate each treated room.

External wall insulation

The job runs for several weeks. Scaffolding is erected around the property. Insulation boards (typically expanded polystyrene or mineral wool) are mechanically fixed and adhesive-bonded to the external walls. A mesh reinforcement layer is applied, followed by a base coat and a finishing render in your chosen colour and texture. Window sills are extended, and all external fittings (pipes, brackets, meters) are refitted. The scaffold comes down, and external areas are tidied.

Combining solid wall insulation with other upgrades

Solid wall insulation delivers the greatest value when combined with other improvements. The insulation reduces your heat demand, which makes every other upgrade more effective and cheaper.

The ideal sequence for a full retrofit: loft insulation first (cheapest, quickest), then solid wall insulation, then draught-proofing, then a heat pump sized to the improved property. Adding solar panels on top provides cheap electricity to run the heat pump, completing the picture.

Frequently asked questions

How much does solid wall insulation cost in the UK?

The Energy Saving Trust puts it at about £12,000 for internal insulation and about £15,000 for external insulation on a typical 3 bed semi. Homes with fewer external walls, such as mid-terraces, cost less, and detached houses more. Use our insulation calculator for your home.

Is solid wall insulation worth the cost?

On bill savings alone at full price, rarely. Homes the government measured used a median 17.3% less gas after solid wall insulation, about £151 a year for a 3 bed semi on gas, so £12,000 would take more than 50 years to pay back. It makes sense when a grant pays for most of it, when you are renovating anyway, or for comfort, damp and a better EPC.

Can I get a grant for solid wall insulation?

Yes, for some households. ECO4 funds solid wall insulation for eligible households on means-tested benefits until 31 December 2026, and councils may fund it through a Warm Homes: Local Grant. Check our grant eligibility tool to see if you qualify.

Does solid wall insulation cause damp?

Badly designed or installed solid wall insulation can, particularly internal insulation, which leaves the old wall colder. Fix existing damp and repairs first, use materials suited to the wall, which for traditional walls usually means vapour open ones, and keep ventilation working. External insulation keeps the wall warm, so its condensation risk is generally lower, but it must be detailed well.

Internal or external: which is better?

External costs more, about £15,000 against £12,000 for a 3 bed semi, but keeps room sizes and avoids cold spots where floors and internal walls meet the external wall. Internal is cheaper, can be done room by room and leaves the outside unchanged, which often suits conservation areas. Listed buildings need consent either way. For most homes the choice comes down to budget, planning and disruption.
