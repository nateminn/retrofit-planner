DIY Loft Insulation: A Step-by-Step Guide for UK Homeowners

The short answer

  • Materials only if you do it yourself, against the £600 to £750 the Energy Saving Trust gives for professional installation

  • A median 3.2% less gas in homes the government measured, about £28 a year for a 3 bed semi on gas
  • Often a day for an easy loft, but call a professional for damp, a flat roof or lots of wiring

Laying loft insulation yourself is one of the few energy upgrades most people can do, and many lofts can be finished in a day. The Energy Saving Trust says it is a DIY job if the loft is easy to get into, free of damp, has regular joists and is not a flat roof. You pay only for materials, against the £600 to £750 the Energy Saving Trust gives for professional installation. The saving is modest: homes the government measured used a median 3.2% less gas after loft insulation, about £28 a year for a 3 bed semi on gas, and a loft with little or nothing in it usually saves more. This guide covers what to buy, how to prepare, step-by-step installation, common mistakes, and when to call a professional instead.

Before you start: is DIY right for your loft?

Yes, if the loft is easy to reach, dry and has visible joists. Call a professional for damp, a flat roof, a converted room, suspected asbestos or lots of wiring, and check for free ECO4 insulation first.

Read the detail

DIY loft insulation works well for accessible, open lofts with joists visible and reasonable headroom. It is a straightforward job that most physically able homeowners can complete in a day.

DIY is suitable when: your loft has a hatch you can climb through, enough headroom to move around (at least in the centre), regular visible joists, and no damp or condensation. Standard mineral wool insulation between and over joists is a DIY-friendly job.

Call a professional when: the loft has damp or condensation problems, you have a flat roof, your loft is a converted room with insulation needed between rafters, you suspect asbestos (see our loft insulation lifespan guide for identifying risk materials), the loft has extensive electrical wiring across the floor, or you have limited mobility and cannot safely move around the space.

Before committing, check whether you can get free loft insulation through ECO4. If you receive qualifying benefits, the whole job can be professionally installed at no cost. Use our grant eligibility checker to find out.

ECO4 closes on 31 December 2026. Work under the scheme has to be done before then, so if you think you qualify, start now. The government has confirmed there will be no successor supplier obligation; instead it has committed £1.5 billion of extra grant funding for households on low incomes. Check what you qualify for.

What you need

100mm rolls between the joists and 170mm rolls across them, to reach 270mm. Add eaves trays, pipe lagging, a tank jacket, hatch insulation and safety kit: an FFP2 or FFP3 mask, goggles and gloves.

Materials

The data behind this table

Measure the loft floor area and check the coverage printed on each roll before you buy. If your loft already has 100mm between the joists, you only need the 170mm cross-layer rolls.

If you already have some insulation at less than 270mm, measure its current depth. You only need to buy enough to top up to the 270mm total. Many homes already have some loft insulation, so you may only need the cross-layer. Check whether your existing insulation is still effective before deciding.

Safety equipment

Mineral wool fibres irritate skin, eyes, and lungs. You must wear: a proper dust mask (FFP2 or FFP3 rated, not a paper dust mask), safety goggles that seal around your eyes, protective gloves, a long-sleeved top and long trousers that you can wash or dispose of afterwards, and sturdy footwear. Work in well-ventilated conditions with the loft hatch open. Do not handle damaged, brittle or old rubber-covered cables: get an electrician to look at them first.

Tools

A utility knife or bread knife (serrated) for cutting rolls to size, a board or piece of plywood to kneel on (never stand between joists on the ceiling plasterboard), a torch or headlamp, a tape measure, and a straightedge for guiding cuts. That is all you need.

Safety first: stay on the joists

The plasterboard ceiling between joists will not support your weight. Always kneel or step on joists, or lay a board across them. Falling through the ceiling can cause serious injury.

Step-by-step installation

Clear the loft, fit eaves trays, lay 100mm between the joists, then 170mm across them. Lag pipes, jacket the tank sides and top, keep cables on top and never lay boards directly on the insulation.

Read the detail

Step 1: Prepare the loft

Clear everything from the loft floor. Move stored items down before you start. If there is existing insulation, check it for damp, mould, or pest damage. Wet or mouldy sections should be removed and replaced, not covered over. Dry, undamaged insulation can stay in place.

Identify and mark the location of any electrical cables on the loft floor. Cables must sit on top of the insulation, not underneath it, to prevent overheating. If cables run through joists or are buried under existing insulation, carefully lift them clear before adding new material.

Check that any recessed downlights in the rooms below have fire-rated covers (also called loft caps) fitted. Standard recessed spotlights can overheat if insulation is packed around them, creating a fire risk. A downlight cover made for the purpose sits over the fitting and keeps the insulation off it, so you can insulate over the top. Fit one to every downlight before you start.

Step 2: Fit eaves ventilation trays

This step is critical and often skipped in DIY jobs. Your loft needs airflow between the eaves (where the roof meets the walls) to prevent condensation. If you push insulation right into the eaves, it blocks this airflow and can cause serious damp problems.

Slide a plastic ventilation tray between the roof felt and the top of the wall plate at each rafter gap along the eaves. The tray holds the insulation back and maintains a clear air channel. Most trays clip into place and do not need fixing.

Step 3: Insulate between the joists (first layer)

If starting from scratch or replacing existing insulation, unroll 100mm mineral wool between the joists. Joists are often 400mm or 600mm apart, and rolls are made to suit these widths. If your joists are wider apart, cut the roll slightly wider than the gap so it wedges in snugly without gaps.

Do not compress the insulation. It should sit loosely between the joists at its natural thickness. Mineral wool works by trapping air, and compressing it defeats the purpose.

Cut pieces to fit around obstacles like pipes, wires (which should sit on top), and the loft hatch opening. Use a long-bladed knife and cut against a straightedge for clean edges. Butt each piece tightly against the next with no gaps.

Step 4: Lay the cross-layer (second layer)

This is where the real thermal benefit comes from. Unroll 170mm mineral wool perpendicular to the joists, covering the joists completely. This eliminates cold bridges through the timber and brings the total depth to 270mm.

Start at the far end of the loft and work back towards the hatch so you do not have to walk over finished areas. Each roll should butt tightly against the previous one with no gaps. Stagger the joints so that seams in the cross-layer do not line up with seams in the between-joist layer.

Do not push insulation into the eaves beyond your ventilation trays. Leave a clear gap for airflow as described in Step 2.

Step 5: Insulate around obstacles

Cold water tanks: Fit an insulating jacket around the sides and top of any water tank in the loft, but do not insulate underneath it. The small amount of heat rising through the ceiling keeps the tank from freezing in winter.

Pipes: Insulate all exposed pipes in the loft with pipe lagging. Once the loft floor is insulated, the space above the insulation becomes colder, increasing the freezing risk for unlagged pipes.

The loft hatch: An uninsulated loft hatch is a major heat leak. Fix a slab of rigid foam insulation (50 to 75mm) to the top of the hatch door with adhesive. Add draught-proofing strip around the hatch frame.

Electrical cables: Lay cables on top of the insulation, not underneath. If cables are buried, gently pull them clear and rest them on the finished surface.

Step 6: Finish up

Once all areas are covered, do a final check. Walk back through on the joists (or your kneeling board) and look for gaps, compressed sections, or areas where insulation has pulled away from edges. Fill any gaps with offcuts.

If you need loft storage, now is the time to install raised loft legs and boarding. The legs lift the boards clear of the full 270mm of insulation; the Energy Saving Trust advises leaving a small ventilation gap between the insulation and the boards to prevent condensation. Never lay boards directly on the insulation.

Shower and wash your work clothes separately after handling mineral wool. The fibres wash out easily but can cause itching if they transfer to other clothing.

Take a photo of the finished job

Photograph the installed insulation with a tape measure showing the depth. This is useful evidence for your next EPC assessment and confirms the depth if you need to make an insurance claim or sell the property.

Common mistakes to avoid

The worst mistake is blocking eaves ventilation, which causes condensation and rot. Also avoid compressing the wool, leaving gaps, insulating under the tank, burying cables and covering downlights without fire-rated caps.

Read the detail

Blocking eaves ventilation. The most common and most damaging mistake. Without airflow, condensation builds up in the loft, wetting the insulation and rotting timber. Always fit ventilation trays.

Compressing insulation with boards or storage. Mineral wool works by trapping air, so squashed insulation loses much of its value. Use raised loft legs if you need storage.

Leaving gaps around edges and obstacles. Heat finds the path of least resistance, and gaps let it bypass the insulation. Fill every gap carefully.

Insulating under the cold water tank. This removes the warmth that keeps the tank from freezing. Insulate sides and top only.

Burying electrical cables. Cables under insulation can overheat. They must sit on top of the finished insulation layer.

Covering recessed lights without fire-rated caps. Standard downlights generate significant heat. Without a proper fire-rated cover, insulation packed around them is a fire hazard. Fit covers before insulating.

What to do after insulating

Next, draught-proof, look at wall insulation and then your heating. Insulating first means a new heat pump or boiler is sized correctly, and a new EPC will show the improvement.

Read the detail

Once your loft is insulated to 270mm, consider these next steps to maximise your energy savings.

Check your EPC. If you are planning to sell or rent the property, a new EPC after insulation will reflect the improvement. Use our EPC improvement planner to estimate the point gain. Landlords should read our EPC guide for landlords for legal requirements.

Draught-proof the rest of the house. With the loft sealed, the next biggest heat losses come from draughts around windows, doors, and floors. Draught excluder strips are an easy DIY fix.

Consider wall insulation. If your home has cavity walls, cavity wall insulation is the next high-value upgrade. For solid walls, read our solid wall insulation cost guide.

Look at your heating system. Better insulation means your home needs less heat to stay warm. If you are thinking about a heat pump or new boiler, insulating first ensures the new system is correctly sized. Use our boiler vs heat pump comparison to see the numbers.

Frequently asked questions

How much does it cost to insulate a loft yourself?

You pay only for materials: mineral wool rolls to cover your loft floor to 270mm, plus eaves trays, pipe lagging, a tank jacket and safety kit (an FFP2 or FFP3 mask, goggles and gloves). Price the rolls once you know your loft floor area. For comparison, the Energy Saving Trust puts professional installation at about £600 for a top-up and £750 from nothing. Use our calculator for the saving.

How long does it take to insulate a loft DIY?

Many straightforward lofts can be done in a day, and a helper passing rolls up through the hatch speeds things up. Lofts with limited headroom, lots of obstacles or existing storage take longer.

What thickness of loft insulation do I need?

The recommended minimum is 270mm of mineral wool. If you already have 100mm between the joists, add a 170mm layer across them. If starting from scratch, lay 100mm between the joists and 170mm across the top. Check whether your existing insulation needs replacing first.

Can I lay new loft insulation over old?

Yes, provided the old insulation is dry and in reasonable condition. Lay new rolls at right angles to the existing layer without compressing it. Remove wet or mouldy material and fix the cause first.

Do I need to board my loft after insulating?

Only if you need storage. If you do, use raised loft legs that lift the boards clear of the full 270mm of insulation, leaving a small ventilation gap between the insulation and the boards. Never lay boards directly on insulation, as squashing it reduces its effectiveness.
