Is Cavity Wall Insulation Worth It? Costs, Savings, and Risks

The short answer

  • About £2,200 for a typical home, and often free through a grant
  • Homes used a median 12.0% less gas afterwards, about £105 a year for a 3 bed semi
  • At full price that takes about 21 years to pay back, so it pays best with a grant
Costs and savingsDo I have cavity walls?DampGet it freeFAQs

Cavity wall insulation costs about £2,200 for a typical home in Great Britain, according to the Energy Saving Trust, and less for a smaller home. When the government compared gas use before and after in 2,191 homes, against similar homes that had nothing done, the median home used 12.0% less gas once its cavity walls were filled. For a 3 bed semi heated by gas that is about £105 a year at October 2026 prices, so at full price the work takes about 21 years to pay back. Many homes get it through a grant, and with a grant it is clearly worth doing, provided a survey confirms your walls are suitable. This guide covers measured savings by home type, the damp risk, how to check your walls, and how to get it free through the ECO4 scheme. Cavity wall insulation is usually the next step after loft insulation.

ECO4 closes on 31 December 2026. Work under the scheme has to be done before then, so if you think you qualify, start now. The government has confirmed there will be no successor supplier obligation; instead it has committed £1.5 billion of extra grant funding for households on low incomes. Check what you qualify for.

How cavity wall insulation works

Installers drill small holes in the outer wall, blow insulation into the cavity and fill the holes. The cavity must be at least 50mm wide and clear of rubble, and a typical job takes about two hours.

Read the detail

Cavity walls consist of two layers of brick or block with a gap (cavity) between them, which needs to be at least 50mm wide and clear of rubble to be filled. This design, usual in UK homes built from the 1920s onwards, was originally intended to prevent rainwater reaching the inner wall. An unfilled cavity also provides some insulation, but far less than filling it with insulating material.

For an average home with easy to reach walls, the Energy Saving Trust says the job usually takes about two hours. The installer drills small holes through the outer wall at regular intervals, blows insulation material into the cavity through the holes, then fills them. The Energy Saving Trust says the material is usually polystyrene beads.

The process is minimally disruptive. No internal work is needed and no rooms are affected. If the filler does not match the brickwork you may still see where the holes were, but they can be painted over. The main inconvenience is the noise of drilling, which lasts a few hours.

Do you have cavity walls?

Probably, if every outside brick shows its long face and the house was built after the 1920s. A brick wall 260mm to 350mm thick is likely to be cavity, and your EPC records the wall type.

Read the detail

Before paying for a survey, you can often identify cavity walls yourself.

Check the brickwork pattern. If all external bricks show their long face (stretcher bond), the walls are almost certainly cavity. If you see alternating long faces and short ends (Flemish bond or English bond), the walls are likely solid. Our solid wall insulation guide covers options for solid-walled homes.

Check the building age. Homes built after the 1920s probably have cavity walls. Older homes are more likely to have solid walls, and some that do have a cavity have one too narrow to fill. Homes built from the 1990s onwards usually had wall insulation fitted when they were built.

Measure wall thickness. Open a window or door and measure the wall from the inside face to the outside face. The Energy Saving Trust says a brick wall between 260mm and 350mm thick is likely to be a cavity wall, and a narrower one is probably solid. Stone walls are usually solid whatever their depth.

Check your EPC. Your EPC report states whether the walls are cavity or solid and whether they are insulated. Check the GOV.UK EPC register. Use our EPC improvement planner to see how filling the cavity would affect your rating.

If you are still unsure, ask a registered installer for a borescope inspection, where a small camera goes through a drilled hole to see whether the cavity is empty or filled.

The damp question

For most UK homes it does not cause damp, but on exposed sites facing driving rain the risk is real. Narrow or rubble filled cavities, damaged brickwork and existing damp also raise it, so insist on a survey first.

Read the detail

The most common concern about cavity wall insulation is whether it causes damp. The short answer: for most UK properties, it does not. But for some properties in specific locations, the risk is real.

Why damp can occur: The original purpose of the cavity was to prevent rainwater from bridging across to the inner wall. Insulation fills this gap. If the external wall is porous, mortar joints are deteriorated, or the property faces prolonged wind-driven rain, moisture can travel through the insulation to the inner wall.

Properties at higher risk: Homes on exposed sites that face driving rain or extreme weather are most at risk. So are homes at risk of flooding, homes with cavities narrower than 50mm or full of rubble, homes with damaged brickwork, pointing or render, and homes with existing damp.

Properties at lower risk: Homes in sheltered locations with a clear cavity of at least 50mm and brickwork, gutters and roofs in good repair. The Energy Saving Trust says these are the homes suited to standard cavity wall insulation.

A competent installer will assess your property's exposure before proceeding. The industry uses a wind-driven rain exposure map to categorise risk. If your property is highly exposed, the installer should either decline the work or propose a fill material certified for that exposure.

Get a proper pre-installation survey

A good installer will inspect the external walls for cracks, damaged mortar, blocked weep holes, and signs of damp before agreeing to fill the cavity. They should also check the cavity width with a borescope. Any repairs to brickwork, pointing, gutters or the roof should be done first. If an installer offers to proceed without any survey, choose a different installer.

What if cavity wall insulation goes wrong?

Call the installer first, and if they will not help, check your paperwork for a CIGA certificate. Work should carry a 25 year CIGA or insurance backed guarantee, and fixes range from repointing to extracting the fill.

Read the detail

If you notice damp or mould after installation, the cause may be the cavity fill, or it may be something else such as poor maintenance made more noticeable. Call the installer first: they should revisit, check the survey and the work, and fix the problem or remove the insulation if the work is at fault. Remedies range from repointing and repairing gutters to extracting the fill, which needs a specialist removal company.

Installation work should come with a 25 year guarantee from the Cavity Insulation Guarantee Agency (CIGA) or an independent insurance backed guarantee. If the installer will not help or no longer exists, check your paperwork for a CIGA certificate: CIGA should then help resolve the problem under the terms of the guarantee. You can check cover on the CIGA website. Make sure any new installation comes with one of these guarantees.

Free cavity wall insulation through ECO4

ECO4 funds it free for many households on qualifying benefits, in D to G homes if owned or E to G if privately rented. ECO4 closes on 31 December 2026 and the work must be finished by then.

Read the detail

The ECO4 scheme funds cavity wall insulation at no cost for many households receiving qualifying benefits (such as Universal Credit, Pension Credit, Child Benefit or Housing Benefit), in homes rated D to G if owned, or E to G if privately rented. Even if you do not receive benefits, your local council may refer you through ECO4 Flex, or, in England, fund the work through a Warm Homes: Local Grant. The Great British Insulation Scheme closed on 31 March 2026.

At about £2,200 and a measured saving of around £100 a year, the full price case is slow. If a grant covers it, there is no reason not to go ahead, as long as the survey is clear. ECO4 closes on 31 December 2026 and the work has to be finished by then. Use our grant eligibility checker.

If you pay yourself, installing insulation in a home is zero rated for VAT until 31 March 2027, and 5% after that (HMRC VAT Notice 708/6).

Cavity wall insulation before a heat pump

Insulate first: a well insulated 3 bed semi needs about a 7.5 kW heat pump against 9 kW at average insulation. At median prices that is about £12,300 against £12,700 installed, before the grant.

Read the detail

If you are planning to install a heat pump, filling cavity walls first reduces your heat demand, which means a smaller heat pump and lower electricity bills. In our model a 3 bed semi needs about a 9 kW heat pump at average insulation and about 7.5 kW when well insulated. At the median price paid under the Boiler Upgrade Scheme that is about £12,700 against £12,300 installed, before the grant.

Combined with loft insulation and draught-proofing, cavity wall insulation prepares your home for the most cost-effective heat pump installation possible. Use our heat pump calculator to see how insulation levels affect sizing and cost.

Frequently asked questions

Is cavity wall insulation worth it?

Usually, if a survey says your walls are suitable, and especially if a grant pays for it. Homes the government measured used a median 12.0% less gas after cavity wall insulation, about £105 a year for a 3 bed semi on gas. At a typical £2,200 that takes about 21 years to pay back at full price, which is why many homes wait for a grant. Homes exposed to driving rain, or with existing damp, need careful assessment first.

How much does cavity wall insulation cost?

The Energy Saving Trust puts it at about £2,200 for a typical home in Great Britain. Smaller homes cost less and larger homes more. The work usually takes about two hours for an average home. ECO4 or a council Warm Homes: Local Grant may cover it for eligible households.

Can cavity wall insulation cause damp?

It should not if it is installed correctly in a suitable wall. The risk is higher where walls face driving rain, where the cavity is narrower than 50mm or full of rubble, where brickwork or pointing is damaged, or where there is existing damp. A survey before installation should check all of these.

How do I know if my walls are cavity or solid?

Look at the brickwork. If every brick shows its long side, the wall is probably a cavity wall. If some bricks are laid end on, showing their short ends, it is probably solid. Most homes built after the 1920s have cavity walls, and a brick wall 260mm to 350mm thick is likely to be one. Your EPC also records the wall type.

Can I get free cavity wall insulation?

Yes, if you qualify. ECO4 funds cavity wall insulation for many households on means-tested benefits until 31 December 2026, and councils run Warm Homes: Local Grants. The Great British Insulation Scheme closed on 31 March 2026. If you pay yourself, installing insulation is zero rated for VAT until 31 March 2027.
