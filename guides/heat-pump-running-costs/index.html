Heat Pump vs Gas Boiler Running Costs in 2026

Heat pump versus gas boiler comes down mostly to the tariff. On a standard electricity tariff, a typical heat pump costs about 7% more to run than a gas boiler, and one in a poorly insulated home about 17% more. On a dedicated heat pump tariff, it costs £150 to £310 less a year across the five homes below. Three things set your own figure: your electricity tariff, your home's insulation and how efficiently the heat pump runs (its COP). This guide gives running costs for five common UK homes, explains the maths and shows how to bring your bills down.

The tariff is everything

On a standard tariff, a heat pump running at 2.78 costs about 7% more to run than gas, and one stuck at 2.55 in a poorly insulated home about 17% more. On a heat pump tariff, it costs 20 to 27% less. Switching to a heat pump tariff is the single most important thing you can do to reduce heat pump running costs. Read our heat pump tariff comparison.

Understanding the maths

The running cost equation is straightforward: divide your annual heat demand by the heat pump's COP, then multiply by your electricity price per kWh.

At the Ofgem price cap for October to December 2026, electricity costs 26.32p/kWh and gas costs 7.97p/kWh, so electricity is 3.3 times the price of gas. But a heat pump running at 2.78 delivers 2.78 kWh of heat for every 1 kWh of electricity. So each kWh of heat from a heat pump costs 26.32p divided by 2.78, which is 9.5p. Gas costs 7.97p per kWh, and a boiler at 90% efficiency turns that into heat at 8.9p per kWh.

On a standard tariff, heat pump heating costs 9.5p per kWh against 8.9p for gas, about 7% more. On a heat pump tariff at an effective rate of 18p/kWh, heat pump heating costs 6.5p per kWh, well below gas.

What is COP and why it matters

COP (coefficient of performance) is the ratio of heat output to electrical input. A COP of 3.0 means the heat pump produces 3 kWh of heat for every 1 kWh of electricity it uses. The higher the COP, the cheaper the heat pump is to run. Measured over a whole year, it is called the seasonal performance factor.

The Electrification of Heat trial for DESNZ monitored 428 air source heat pumps in British homes. Counting the heat pump, its backup heater and pumps, the median home got 2.78 units of heat per unit of electricity over a year, and the middle half fell between 2.55 and 3.05. Efficiency moves with the weather: at 10°C outside the median heat pump ran at 3.37, and at minus 3°C it fell to 2.37. The trial had too few ground source heat pumps to give a reliable typical figure, though the ones it monitored stayed above 3.0 even at minus 5°C.

How to reduce your heat pump running costs

1. Switch to a heat pump tariff

This is the biggest single lever. Tariffs like Cosy Octopus charge less for electricity in set windows, in its case 4am to 7am, 1pm to 4pm and 10pm to midnight. Because a heat pump can warm the house and the hot water cylinder in those windows, much of your heating moves to the cheaper rate. An effective rate of around 18p/kWh is realistic, roughly a third below the 26.32p cap rate. Read our full comparison of heat pump tariffs.

2. Insulate your home first

Every improvement to your insulation reduces the electricity the heat pump uses. Loft insulation costs about £750 fitted from scratch, or £600 to top up from 120mm, and cavity wall insulation about £2,700 for a typical home, according to the Energy Saving Trust. In homes that had them fitted in 2023 to 2024, the median measured gas saving was 12.0% for cavity wall insulation and 3.2% for loft insulation (DESNZ NEED). For older homes with solid walls, solid wall insulation gave the biggest measured cut, a median 17.3%, and is also the most expensive. Use our insulation calculator for your property.

3. Run the heat pump at low flow temperatures

Heat pumps are most efficient when the water they send to the radiators is cool. In the Electrification of Heat trial, the median heat pump ran at 3.50 while producing water at 35°C, but only 2.38 at 55°C. Running hot to make up for undersized radiators costs you every year, so if your installer recommends radiator upgrades, weigh their cost against the running cost they save.

4. Use the timer and thermostat correctly

Heat pumps work best when run for longer periods at lower output rather than in short bursts at high output. Set a steady temperature and let the heat pump hold it. Avoid turning it right up for a quick blast of heat.

5. Add solar panels

Solar panels generate electricity during daylight hours, some of which can power the heat pump directly. With a solar diverter or smart controls, you can prioritise heating hot water when solar generation is highest. Read Are Solar Panels Worth It? for the full financial analysis.

If your home is off the gas grid and currently heated by oil or LPG, the economics of a heat pump are much stronger even on a standard tariff.

For homes on oil or LPG, a heat pump on a standard tariff already cuts the cost of heat by about 11% against oil and about 15% against LPG. On a heat pump tariff the cut is about 39% against oil and 42% against LPG. For homes on direct electric heating such as panel heaters or an electric boiler, a heat pump cuts the cost of heat by about 64% on a standard tariff and 75% on a heat pump tariff.

Monitoring your actual costs

Once your heat pump is installed, track actual consumption to verify it matches expectations. Many heat pump systems also have built-in monitoring through their smartphone apps, showing COP, energy consumed, and heat delivered.

If your heat pump's efficiency stays below about 2.5 over a season, it is in the bottom quarter of the homes in the Electrification of Heat trial, and something may be wrong with the installation, the system settings or your home's insulation. Contact your installer to investigate. Common issues include flow temperatures set too high, poor refrigerant charge, and undersized radiators forcing the system to work harder.

Frequently asked questions

Are heat pumps cheaper to run than gas boilers?

On a heat pump tariff, yes: about 20 to 27% cheaper in the five homes on this page. On a standard tariff, a typical heat pump costs about 7% more to run than a gas boiler, and one in a poorly insulated home about 17% more. For a 3-bed semi with average insulation, a year of heating costs about £877 with gas, £937 with a heat pump on the standard 26.32p rate and £641 on an 18p heat pump tariff.

How much does a heat pump cost to run per year?

It depends on the size of the home, its insulation, how efficiently the heat pump runs and your tariff. A 3-bed semi with average insulation, with the heat pump running at the typical measured efficiency of 2.78, costs about £937 a year on a standard tariff or £641 on an 18p heat pump tariff. The same house with poor insulation costs about £1,134 on a standard tariff, because it needs more heat and the heat pump runs less efficiently, at 2.55. Use our heat pump calculator for your own home.

What is COP and why does it matter?

COP (coefficient of performance) is the heat a heat pump delivers for each unit of electricity it uses. A COP of 3.0 means 3 kWh of heat for every 1 kWh of electricity, so the higher it is, the lower your bills. Measured over a whole year it is called the seasonal performance factor. In the Electrification of Heat trial, which monitored 428 air source heat pumps in British homes, the median was 2.78 and the middle half of homes fell between 2.55 and 3.05.

Do I need a special electricity tariff for a heat pump?

No, but it is the biggest single saving. Heat pump tariffs such as Cosy Octopus charge less for electricity in set hours of the day. If most of your heating runs in those hours, your average price can fall from the 26.32p standard rate to around 18p per kWh, about a third less. See our tariff guide.

How does insulation affect heat pump running costs?

Better insulation cuts the heat your home needs and lets the heat pump run more efficiently. For a 4-bed detached house, our model puts heat demand at about 14,585 kWh a year with poor insulation and 11,169 kWh with good insulation, 23% less. Because the heat pump also runs more efficiently (3.05 rather than 2.55), the standard tariff running cost falls from about £1,505 to £964, a drop of 36%. Loft and cavity wall insulation are the usual first steps.
