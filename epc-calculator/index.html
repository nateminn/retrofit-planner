How this calculator estimates your rating

An EPC score runs from 1 to 100 and is calculated from the modelled cost of heating, hot water and lighting per square metre. Our estimate starts from the average score for homes of your property's age in the English Housing Survey, where the mean SAP rating for English dwellings was 68 points in 2024, and 27% of homes built before 1919 reached bands A to C against 55% to 94% of homes built after 1944. It then adds or subtracts points for your walls, loft, glazing, heating, solar panels and lighting using the same point ranges as the improvement table below. Expect it to land within about 6 points of an assessor's figure. It is not an EPC: a certificate needs a domestic energy assessor, costs about £65 to £95 and lasts ten years. Read how EPC points are calculated or see what each band pays in bills.

How is an EPC calculated?

An Energy Performance Certificate is not a reading of the energy you actually used last year. It is a model. A domestic energy assessor records the fabric and services of the property, and the government's Standard Assessment Procedure estimates what the home would cost to run under a standard heating pattern, expressed per square metre of floor area.

The rating covers regulated energy only: space heating, water heating, cooling, ventilation and lighting. Cooking and appliances are excluded by design, which is why two households in identical homes can run up very different bills and still sit in the same band. The calculation also uses fixed fuel prices rather than live tariffs, so your rating does not move when the price cap does.

Which methodology is used

Existing homes are assessed using RdSAP, the reduced data version of SAP that lets an assessor work from a site visit rather than construction drawings. GOV.UK lists SAP 10.3 and RdSAP 10.0.1 as the current approved versions. The government plans to replace SAP with the Home Energy Model, but GOV.UK states that SAP 10.3 is currently the sole approved methodology and gives no start date, so every EPC issued today still comes from SAP. Separately, reformed EPCs will add new headline metrics covering fabric performance, the heating system and smart readiness, with the existing A to G efficiency rating kept alongside them as a legacy metric for a period.

EPC rating calculation: turning points into a band

The model returns a single score between 1 and 100, where 1 is extremely poor efficiency and 100 represents a home with no net annual energy cost. That score is then placed in one of seven bands, shown in the chart above. The bands are not equal widths, so a single point is worth more at some thresholds than others. Going from 68 to 69 lifts you out of D and into C. Going from 60 to 68 is eight points of work that leaves you in band D throughout.

That is why this calculator ranks upgrades by cost per point rather than by headline saving. If you sit two points below a band boundary, £100 to £300 of LED lighting can move your band, while £3,000 to £7,000 of new glazing spent mid-band may not. For how each element is weighted, read how EPC points are calculated.

Where most homes land

In 2024 the average SAP rating for English dwellings was 68 points, up from 45 points in 1996. Band C is now the most common rating, covering 49% of owner occupied homes, 48% of privately rented homes and 69% of social rented homes. Across the whole stock, 56% of dwellings sit in bands A to C, 35% in band D and 9% in bands E to G. Age remains the strongest single predictor: 27% of homes built before 1919 reach bands A to C, against 55% to 94% of homes built after 1944.

How to improve your EPC rating in 2026

Your EPC (Energy Performance Certificate) rates your home's energy efficiency on a scale of A (most efficient) to G (least efficient). Band C is now the most common rating in England, and band D the next most common, so most homes are working from the middle of the scale rather than the bottom. Improving your rating reduces energy bills, increases property value, and is increasingly required by law for rental properties.

The most cost-effective improvements depend on what your property already has. A home with no loft insulation should start there. It's cheap and adds 5 to 10 EPC points. A home that already has good insulation but an old gas boiler would benefit most from a heat pump, which adds 10 to 15 points.

EPC improvements ranked by cost-effectiveness

Based on Energy Saving Trust and BRE data, here are typical improvements ranked by EPC points gained per pound spent:

  1. Loft insulation top-up: £250 to £400, adds 3 to 7 points. Best value if your loft has less than 100mm of insulation. Often available free through grants.
  2. Loft insulation (from zero): £300 to £600, adds 5 to 10 points. Calculate your savings.
  3. Cavity wall insulation: £400 to £1,500, adds 10 to 15 points. Check if your walls are suitable.
  4. LED lighting throughout: £100 to £300, adds 2 to 5 points. Simple DIY improvement.
  5. Smart heating controls: £150 to £400, adds 2 to 4 points.
  6. Double glazing: £3,000 to £7,000, adds 5 to 10 points. Expensive but improves comfort significantly.
  7. Heat pump: £7,000 to £13,000 before £7,500 grant, adds 10 to 15 points. Calculate your costs.
  8. Solar panels: £5,000 to £8,000 for a 4kW system, adds about 7 points. Calculate your ROI.
  9. Solid wall insulation: £4,500 to £8,000 done internally, adds about 20 points. Most impactful single measure but expensive. Check potential savings.

EPC requirements for landlords

Rental properties in England and Wales must currently have a minimum EPC rating of Band E. Under the rules in force today, a local authority can fine a landlord up to £5,000 in total per property.

That changes on 1 October 2030. In its January 2026 response to the privately rented homes consultation, the government confirmed a single compliance date for all tenancies, dropping the earlier plan to phase the standard in for new tenancies first. Homes will need to meet a primary standard on the fabric performance metric plus a secondary standard on either the heating system metric or the smart readiness metric, whichever the landlord prefers. The maximum fine rises to £30,000 per property per breach.

Landlords will be required to spend up to £10,000 per property on qualifying improvements. If you reach that cap and the property still falls short, you can register a cost cap exemption, which lasts 10 years. Most other exemptions last five years. Improving gradually costs less than rushing at the deadline, which is what the cost-per-point ranking above is for.

Frequently asked questions

How is an EPC calculated?

An assessor records the property's walls, roof, floors, windows, heating, hot water, lighting and any renewables, then the government's Standard Assessment Procedure models what the home would cost to run under a standard heating pattern. The result is a score from 1 to 100, which is then banded A to G. It counts space heating, water heating, cooling, ventilation and lighting only, so cooking and appliances do not affect it.

Is this Energy Performance Certificate calculator free?

Yes. It is free to use, runs in your browser and needs no sign-up or email address. It gives you an estimate rather than a certificate: only an accredited domestic energy assessor can issue an EPC, and a certificate is valid for 10 years.

What is the minimum EPC rating for landlords in 2026?

Band E. That is the standard in force today, backed by fines of up to £5,000 per property. From 1 October 2030 every privately rented home in England and Wales must meet a Band C equivalent standard, and the maximum fine rises to £30,000 per property per breach. Use the calculator above to find the cheapest route there.

How accurate is the EPC estimate?

Usually within 6 points of an assessor's figure, which is enough to tell you the band you are likely in and how far you are from the next one. The estimate uses the average score for homes of your age from the English Housing Survey and adjusts it for your insulation, heating and glazing. Only a domestic energy assessor can issue an EPC.

How much does it cost to improve an EPC rating?

Simple measures like loft insulation (£300 to £600) can improve your rating by 5 to 10 points. More significant improvements like a heat pump or solid wall insulation cost £5,000 to £15,000 but can improve ratings by 15 to 30 points. Many improvements qualify for government grants.

What improves an EPC rating the most?

The most impactful single measure is typically wall insulation (cavity or solid), which can improve an EPC by 10 to 25 points. Installing a heat pump can add 10 to 15 points. Loft insulation adds 5 to 10 points. The best approach depends on what your property already has.

Does a new boiler improve EPC rating?

Replacing an old gas boiler with a modern condensing boiler can add 5 to 10 EPC points. However, replacing it with a heat pump adds 10 to 15 points because the EPC methodology values renewable heat sources more highly. See our boiler vs heat pump comparison for the full analysis.
