Heat Pump for a 1960s House: Cost, Cavity Walls and Savings in 2026

An air source heat pump for a 1960s estate house, the three bedroom semi or terrace built in the post-war housing boom, costs about £12,700 installed for a typical semi, the median installers reported under the Boiler Upgrade Scheme in 2025/26 for the 9 kW unit it needs, with most jobs costing £11,000 to £15,500. That is the whole job as installers recorded it for the grant, cylinder, labour and VAT included; some homes also need larger radiators, and the £7,500 Boiler Upgrade Scheme grant brings it to about £5,200. One feature decides much of the rest: this house has cavity walls, and an empty cavity can be filled. On our model, taking the house from EPC band E to band C, which usually means the cavity plus the loft and draughts, cuts heat demand from 10,989 kWh to 8,415 kWh, a cut of about 23%, and takes the heat pump from 10 kW to 7.5 kW. It is why a 1960s estate house is one of the easier heat pump candidates in the British housing stock and a Victorian terrace with solid walls is one of the harder ones. Running cost as most of these houses stand is £705 a year on a heat pump tariff against £877 for gas, but £937 on standard electricity, which is £60 more than the boiler it replaces. The tariff is a condition of the case, not a bonus on top of it.

What a 1960s house heat pump really costs

Across every house type in England and Wales, the median air source heat pump installation funded by the Boiler Upgrade Scheme cost £12,908 in April to June 2026, with a quarter of jobs under £11,128 and a quarter over £15,627 (DESNZ Boiler Upgrade Scheme statistics, Table Q1.1A). A typical 1960s three bedroom semi sits just under that median. It is a regular house to work on: squared-off rooms, lower ceilings than a Victorian house, often an airing cupboard that still takes a cylinder, and side access wide enough to carry a monobloc unit to the back garden without going through the hall.

What moves the price inside the range is the wall. A 1960s cavity that has never been filled loses roughly twice the heat of the same wall filled, and that shows up as a larger unit, more radiators to change and a bigger bill every winter.

The cavity wall is the whole story

Cavity construction was normal by the 1960s: two leaves of brick or block with a gap between them, tied across. The gap was there to stop rain crossing to the inner leaf, not to hold insulation, and it was usually left empty. The RdSAP 10 assessment method gives an empty cavity of this age a default of 1.5 W/m²K and a filled one 0.7 (BRE), roughly half the heat loss. DESNZ estimates that 71% of the 21.8 million homes with cavity walls in Great Britain now have them insulated, and that about 3.7 million still have easy to treat empty cavities (Household Energy Efficiency statistics, March 2026). If yours is one of them, the improvement is sitting inside the walls.

The installer drills small holes through the outer leaf, injects mineral wool fibre or bonded beads under pressure and repoints to match. Nothing happens inside the house and the job is usually done in a day. The Energy Saving Trust puts the cost at around £2,700 for a typical home in Great Britain (updated 10 February 2026). Our cavity wall insulation guide covers the wind-driven rain question and how to tell a cavity wall from a solid one.

What it buys, measured in real homes: those that had cavity wall insulation in 2023 used a median 12.0% less gas the following year, about 1,208 kWh or £96 at the October 2026 gas price (DESNZ NEED impact of measures, June 2026). That is the largest measured saving for any measure a cavity wall house can have, though some homes save more and many under-heated homes take part of the gain as warmth. Done before a heat pump it also cuts the peak heat loss, so the installer designs around a smaller unit and a lower flow temperature. On our model, moving from band E to band C, the cavity plus the loft and draughts, takes the heat pump from 10 kW to 7.5 kW and the seasonal efficiency our model assumes from 2.55 to 3.05, about 20% better, on top of a 23% cut in heat demand.

Do the cavity first, then get quotes

On our model, taking this house from band E to band C before the survey takes £1,000 off the median installed price and £307 a year off a heat pump tariff bill. Get it done first, so the design is built around the house you will be living in. If you receive a qualifying benefit, ECO4 may pay for it outright, but ECO4 closes on 31 December 2026 with no successor supplier obligation, so the work has to be finished by then. In England, households on a low income can also ask their council about the Warm Homes: Local Grant; the Great British Insulation Scheme closed on 31 March 2026. What else a 1960s house brings

The cavity is the headline, but four other features of post-war estate housing turn up on every survey.

Large windows, usually already replaced

Post-war design opened the elevations up, so a 1960s living room often has a picture window running most of the front wall. Almost all of it was single glazed timber originally and most of it has since been replaced, which is why the middle row of our table is the common case. Watch for sealed units whose seals have failed, which show as misting between the panes, and for original frames left in a back bedroom or porch. The Energy Saving Trust puts the saving from replacing single glazing with A-rated double glazing at about £85 a year for a semi (May 2026).

Loft insulation that started thin

Lofts in the early 1960s were often built with little or no insulation. Most have been topped up at least once, but many still sit below the 270mm that counts as done. The Energy Saving Trust puts a professional top-up from 120mm to 270mm at about £600 (May 2026), less as a weekend job, and homes that had loft insulation fitted or topped up in 2023 used a median 3.2% less gas the following year. See our loft insulation guide and DIY loft insulation.

Floors: solid concrete or suspended timber

The ground floor is a coin toss. Solid concrete slabs became common in this period and are hard to insulate without raising or digging out the floor, so in practice they usually stay as they are, which is also why underfloor heating retrofits rarely make sense here. Suspended timber over a ventilated void is the other half of the stock and can be insulated between the joists; the Energy Saving Trust puts a typical suspended floor job at around £4,700, saving about £70 a year (August 2025). It pairs well with lifting boards for new pipework. See our underfloor insulation costs.

Draughts and small bore pipework

Air leakage concentrates in three places: the loft hatch, the gaps where suspended floorboards meet the skirting, and any unused chimney left from a back boiler or open fire. The Energy Saving Trust puts professional draught-proofing of a whole house's windows and doors at around £250, and our draught-proofing guide covers the rest. Pipework is the real trap. A house re-plumbed in the 1970s or 1980s may have 8mm or 10mm microbore, which can restrict flow at heat pump temperatures; Octopus says its Cosy heat pumps work with microbore of 10mm or larger, and otherwise the circuits that matter are replaced. Ask the installer to lift a board at the survey, not on installation day.

What size heat pump for a 1960s house

On our model a typical 1960s three bedroom semi takes a 9 kW air source heat pump, rising to 10 kW with the cavities open and a thin loft, and falling to 7.5 kW once they are filled and the loft is at 270mm. That is a smaller unit, a cheaper installation and a quieter machine in the side passage. Our heat pump sizing guide explains how the size is worked out.

What decides it for your house is the room-by-room heat loss calculation your MCS installer carries out at the design outdoor temperature for your location. The figure to check on this house is the wall U-value. A surveyor who enters a filled cavity figure when yours is open, or an empty cavity figure when yours was filled years ago, gets the whole design wrong in one line. Ask what value was used and how it was established: an EPC saying "cavity wall, as built, no insulation" is a starting point, not proof, because assessors often assume rather than test.

Oversizing is the common failure, and it happens when the installer sizes from the old boiler output. A boiler, especially a combi, is sized for hot water flow rate rather than for the building, so its output is usually far above the house's heat loss. A heat pump that is too large switches on and off instead of running steadily, which costs efficiency and wears the compressor. In the Boiler Upgrade Scheme data each kilowatt less is worth roughly £250 to £650 off the median installed price.

Radiators, cylinder and pipework

A heat pump delivers water at 40°C to 50°C rather than the 65°C to 75°C a gas boiler usually runs at, so each radiator has to be larger to give off the same heat. A 1960s semi usually needs fewer changes than a Victorian terrace at the same design temperature, and the work is part of the installed price rather than an extra. Double panel radiators in the same wall space usually solve it without moving pipework. Our radiator sizing guide explains how installers decide which ones change.

The hot water cylinder is where this house quietly saves you money. Many 1960s semis were built with a tank in an airing cupboard on the landing, and even where a combi went in later the cupboard is often still there, still plumbed and big enough for a heat pump cylinder; the Energy Saving Trust says a cylinder will usually fit a cupboard of at least 80 cm by 80 cm. That keeps the cylinder work simpler than in a flat or small terrace where the cylinder has nowhere to go. An existing cylinder still usually needs replacing, because heat pump cylinders have a much larger coil to transfer heat at lower temperatures.

Where microbore is found, replacing the primaries and the runs to the radiators that change is normally enough. There is rarely any need to re-plumb a whole 1960s house.

Why a leaky house is the worse case, not the better one

There is a comfortable idea in circulation that the worse your house is, the more a heat pump saves you, because there is more waste to recover. Our model says the opposite, and why matters, because it changes what you do first.

A leaky house does need more heat, and more heat means a larger bill for whatever system you use. But a heat pump is not a fixed-efficiency machine. It has to push heat in at whatever flow temperature the radiators and the fabric demand, and a cold draughty house demands a higher one. The Electrification of Heat trial found heat pump efficiency falls steadily as flow temperature rises, and our model reflects that by using 2.55 at band E against 3.05 at band C. A gas boiler loses much less to the same conditions.

The two effects pull against each other and, on our model, the efficiency one wins. Against gas on a heat pump tariff, the band E house saves £120 a year, the band D house £172 and the band C house £199: the saving gets larger as the house gets better, not worse. On a standard tariff the pattern is sharper. The band E house is £161 a year worse off, the band D house £60 worse off, and only the band C house comes out ahead, by £19. One caution: the same trial found no significant difference in performance by the age of the house where an installer had judged it suitable, so the band E figures are a cautious case rather than a forecast.

The practical rule is the one every good installer gives: fabric first, and on this house fabric means the cavity. Do not fit a heat pump to a 1960s house with open cavities and expect it to compensate. It will run hotter, cost more and give you a worse experience of the technology than the house is capable of. The Boiler Upgrade Scheme for a 1960s house

The Boiler Upgrade Scheme pays £7,500 towards an air source or ground source heat pump in England and Wales. Until March 2027 it pays £9,000 where the home is heated by oil or LPG and has no mains gas connection (GOV.UK). Almost every 1960s estate house is on mains gas, so the £7,500 rate is the one that applies. The exception is a village-edge development of the same era that the gas grid never reached, where an oil boiler and the £9,000 rate make the arithmetic far more attractive, provided the work is done before the higher rate ends.

Your installer applies, and since April 2026 the installer must take the grant off the price quoted to you. The scheme runs to 2030 and installations carry 0% VAT until March 2027. You need to own the house and be replacing fossil fuel or electric heating. An unfilled cavity has not blocked the grant since May 2024, and since April 2026 you no longer need a valid EPC to apply (Boiler Upgrade Scheme (Amendment) Regulations 2026). That is a policy decision rather than a technical one: you can take the grant with open cavities, and on the figures above you should still fill them. Read our BUS grant guide for the rules, our grant eligibility checker for anything else you qualify for, and our Warm Homes Plan guide for how insulation funding changes from 2027.

Is a heat pump worth it for a 1960s house

Against a new gas boiler, about £3,500 for a three bedroom home according to DESNZ, including VAT, a typical 1960s semi pays £5,200 for the heat pump after the grant, about £1,700 more. At £172 a year on a heat pump tariff that is a payback of about 10 years, or about 12 if 5% VAT returns to electricity in April 2027, against the 20 years the government assumes a heat pump lasts and 15 for a gas boiler (Boiler Upgrade Scheme impact assessment, 2022). The spread is the honest part: a job at the cheap end, where the airing cupboard takes the cylinder, the radiators are already generous and the pipework is 15mm, pays for itself well inside the life of the equipment, and at the bottom of the range, £3,500 after the grant, it costs no more than the boiler. A job at the top of the range, £8,000 after the grant, is £4,500 more and takes about 26 years.

On a standard electricity tariff there is no payback at all as the house typically stands, because the heat pump costs £60 a year more to run than the boiler. That is the sentence most guides leave out. For a 1960s semi on mains gas this is marginal on running cost alone and is really a choice about getting off gas: the carbon, the long horizon, and the removal of a combustion appliance from the house. If you are on a heat pump tariff and you fill the cavity, the numbers work. If you will not move tariff, they do not, and you should know that before you sign.

Two things improve the case. Capping the gas supply saves a further £108 a year in standing charge. And if the boiler is due for replacement anyway, the comparison is not the heat pump against nothing; it is about £5,200 against the £3,500 or so you were going to spend regardless. Noise, placement and the estate layout

Post-war estates were often laid out with space around the houses, and that is worth real money here. A 1960s semi normally has a side passage or driveway alongside it and a garden behind, so the outdoor unit can sit on the flank wall away from the party wall, or at the rear beside the kitchen, with a short pipe run either way. A monobloc outdoor unit can weigh 100 kg or more, and it goes round the side rather than through the hall on a trolley.

The rule to satisfy in England is MCS 020(a), a sound calculation that must show 37 dB or less at one metre from the nearest habitable room window or door of each neighbour (MCS); Wales moved to the same MCS 020(a) test on 1 June 2026 (MCS). The one metre boundary requirement left permitted development in May 2025, so the sound calculation is now the test that matters. At one metre the Grant Aerona 290 range runs from 40 to 46 dB(A) and the Ecodan Ultra Quiet 8.5 kW is 45 dB(A), and most 1960s plots pass easily because the gap between houses is wider than on a terraced street. Two placements cause trouble: an internal corner, which the MCS 020(a) method treats as three reflecting surfaces and scores 3 dB higher than a single wall, and a unit squeezed against a fence line on a narrow plot. Where neither can be avoided, a quieter unit or an acoustic enclosure is the answer.

Planning permission is not normally needed. An air source heat pump on a house in England is permitted development if the sound calculation passes, the unit is no more than 1.5 cubic metres, it is the only heat pump on a house that is not detached, and the house is not listed; in a conservation area it cannot go on a wall or roof facing a road (GPDO Class G). Few 1960s estates are in conservation areas, though some architecturally significant ones are, so it is worth a check. Our planning permission guide covers the exceptions. Tell your neighbour before the survey rather than after the unit arrives.

If the unit does end up on a plinth against a wall you share, anti-vibration pads under the feet stop the hum carrying into next door's bedroom.

Getting quotes: what to check

Get three quotes from MCS-certified installers and compare them on the design rather than the headline price. For this house in particular, each quote should include:

  • A room-by-room heat loss calculation with the design outdoor temperature and the wall U-value it used: the RdSAP 10 defaults for this age are 0.7 W/m²K for a filled cavity and 1.5 for an empty one.
  • Evidence of how the cavity was assessed, ideally a borescope check rather than a look at the EPC.
  • The design flow temperature, ideally 50°C or lower, and the seasonal performance the installer expects at that temperature, in writing.
  • A radiator schedule listing every emitter, which ones change and their new sizes.
  • The cylinder size, and confirmation that it goes back into the existing airing cupboard or a price for building one.
  • A pipe size survey, with a note of any microbore found and what it will cost to replace.
  • An MCS 020(a) sound calculation for the exact outdoor unit position with each neighbour's nearest window or door marked.
  • The unit and installation warranties, and who you call when something fails.
  • Confirmation that the Boiler Upgrade Scheme grant is already taken off the quoted price, as the scheme now requires.

Most installations here take a few days, longer if most radiators are swapped or microbore is replaced. Find installers through the MCS installer search. If your estate was built in no-fines concrete, precast reinforced concrete or timber frame, say so on the first phone call: the cavity story in this guide does not apply to you and the survey has to start somewhere else.

How much does a heat pump cost to run in a 1960s house?

At the Ofgem price cap for October to December 2026, a 1960s semi using 9,900 kWh of heat costs £705 a year on a heat pump tariff, £937 on standard electricity and £877 on gas, so on a standard tariff the heat pump costs £60 a year more than the gas boiler it replaces. At EPC band C, with the cavities filled, the figures are £546, £726 and £745, and that is the only version of this house that beats gas without a special tariff. See our running costs guide.

Is a heat pump worth it for a 1960s house?

On a heat pump tariff, usually: you save £172 a year against gas as the house typically stands and £199 at band C, so the roughly £1,700 extra over a new gas boiler pays back in about 10 years at the medians. On a standard electricity tariff there is no saving, so the tariff is a condition rather than a bonus. For most 1960s houses on mains gas this is marginal on running cost alone and is really a choice about getting off gas. Compare with our boiler vs heat pump tool.

Can every 1960s house have its cavity walls filled?

No. Houses built in no-fines concrete, precast reinforced concrete systems such as Airey and Cornish, and many timber frame houses have no cavity that can be injected, and fill is either impossible or harmful in them. Narrow or rubble-filled cavities and walls exposed to severe wind-driven rain are also usually ruled out. A borescope inspection settles it before you commit to the rest of the plan.
