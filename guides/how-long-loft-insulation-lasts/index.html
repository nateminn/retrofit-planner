How Long Does Loft Insulation Last?

The short answer

  • Around 40 years for correctly installed loft insulation, and mineral wool keeps working while it stays dry and uncompressed
  • Replace it only if it is wet, mouldy, badly compressed or damaged by pests; otherwise top it up to 270mm
  • About £600 for a professional top-up from 120mm, saving about £30 a year for a 3 bed detached house on gas

The Energy Saving Trust says correctly installed loft insulation has a lifespan of around 40 years. Mineral wool (glass wool or rock wool rolls), the usual material, keeps working for as long as it stays dry, uncompressed and undisturbed. Loose fill materials can settle and thin out over time, and spray foam brings its own risks for roof timbers and has caused problems with some mortgages. This guide covers how each type ages, how to tell if yours needs attention, and what to do about it.

The 40 year figure is the Energy Saving Trust's for correctly installed loft insulation. For other materials, check the manufacturer's guidance. Real performance depends on installation quality, ventilation and keeping the loft dry.

Signs your loft insulation needs attention

Most homeowners never check their loft insulation after installation. A quick inspection every few years can identify problems before they cost you serious money in wasted heating. Here is what to look for.

Depth has dropped below 270mm

The recommended minimum depth for mineral wool is 270mm, the depth building regulations expect in new homes. Many older lofts have much less. Topping up is worth doing, though the measured saving is modest: homes the government measured used a median 3.2% less gas after loft insulation, about £30 a year for a 3 bed detached house on gas. Insulation is compressed or flattened

Mineral wool works by trapping air in tiny pockets between fibres. If you have stored boxes, boards, or heavy items directly on top of the insulation, those sections are compressed and have lost most of their thermal value. This is one of the most common problems in UK lofts. If you use your loft for storage, you need raised loft legs and boarding that sits above the full depth of insulation without squashing it.

Visible damp, water staining, or mould

Wet insulation is useless insulation. Water conducts heat more than 20 times better than air, so even a small damp patch creates a major cold bridge. Common causes include roof leaks, burst pipes, condensation from inadequate ventilation, and overflow from water tanks stored in the loft.

Fix the source first. Mineral wool that got slightly wet from a problem that is now fixed may recover once fully dry, but if it stays damp, is matted flat or smells musty, replace it. Replace wet loose fill and anything mouldy. If the loft has a damp problem, the Energy Saving Trust advises getting professional advice before adding any insulation.

Pest damage or nesting

Rodents, birds, and squirrels tear loft insulation apart to create nesting material. Look for tunnels, droppings, scattered fibres, and gaps where insulation has been pulled away. Address the pest issue first by sealing entry points, then replace damaged sections.

Rooms below the loft feel cold

If upstairs bedrooms are consistently colder than ground floor rooms, or if you notice icicles forming on the eaves in winter, your loft insulation is likely inadequate. Cold spots on upstairs ceilings visible on a thermal imaging camera confirm this. Quick loft inspection checklist

Measure the depth in several locations (aim for 270mm). Check for damp patches, especially around roof edges and any pipes. Look for pest droppings or disturbed areas. Feel whether the insulation is fluffy or compressed. If you have doubts, check your EPC certificate for the loft insulation rating.

Topping up vs replacing loft insulation

In most cases, you do not need to rip out old loft insulation. The standard approach is to top up by laying new mineral wool rolls perpendicular to the existing layer.

Top up when: the existing insulation is dry, clean, and less than 270mm deep. Simply add new rolls on top, running them at 90 degrees to the joists to cover any gaps.

Replace when: the existing material is wet, mouldy or pest-infested. Old loose fill that may contain asbestos needs testing first, and removal by a specialist if it does. Full removal and replacement costs more but ensures you start from a clean, effective base.

You can install loft insulation yourself and pay only for materials, or have it professionally installed: the Energy Saving Trust puts that at about £600 to top up from 120mm and £750 from nothing. If you receive qualifying benefits, the ECO4 scheme may cover the full cost. Check our grant eligibility tool to see if you qualify.

ECO4 closes on 31 December 2026. Work under the scheme has to be done before then, so if you think you qualify, start now. Does old insulation contain asbestos?

Most common loft insulation materials, including glass wool, rock wool, and cellulose, do not contain asbestos. However, there are two situations where caution is needed.

Loose fill, including vermiculite: The Health and Safety Executive says asbestos may be present in any home built or refurbished before 2000, including loose asbestos in ceiling and wall cavities, and some old vermiculite loft insulation may contain it too. You cannot tell by looking. If your loft has old loose granules, do not disturb them: have a sample tested by an accredited laboratory before doing anything else.

Asbestos board and cement: The Health and Safety Executive also lists asbestos insulating board and asbestos cement water tanks among the places asbestos is still found. These are separate from loft insulation but can be in the loft space. If you suspect either, leave it undisturbed and get professional advice.

Never disturb suspected asbestos yourself

If you find any material in your loft that you cannot identify, or if your home was built or refurbished before 2000 and has loose fill insulation, get it tested before touching it. Asbestos fibres are only dangerous when disturbed and inhaled.

Spray foam insulation: a word of caution

Spray foam applied to the underside of the roof has been sold as a quick fix, but it carries risks. The Energy Saving Trust warns that roof timbers can rot if the roof leaks or condensation forms, and does not recommend spraying foam onto a damaged or leaking roof instead of repairing it. Some mortgage lenders and surveyors have been wary of homes with spray foam, because it can hide roof defects and is difficult to remove, although the Energy Saving Trust says this should now be less of an issue where it was installed correctly.

If you are considering loft insulation purely for energy savings and are not converting the loft into a living space, mineral wool between and over the joists is the safer, cheaper, and more effective choice. If your loft already has spray foam and you are concerned, check with your mortgage lender and consider getting a surveyor's opinion.

Making your existing insulation last longer

Good loft insulation can last decades with minimal maintenance. These practices protect your investment:

Maintain roof integrity. Fix missing or slipped tiles promptly. Even a small roof leak can saturate a large area of insulation over one winter season. Check the roof from outside after storms and from inside the loft for daylight showing through gaps.

Ensure adequate ventilation. Lofts need airflow to prevent condensation. Do not block eaves vents with insulation. Use eaves ventilation trays where insulation meets the roof slope to maintain the air gap. This is critical and often overlooked in DIY installations.

Keep the loft clear or use raised boarding. If you must use your loft for storage, fit loft legs that lift the boards clear of the full 270mm of insulation, with a small ventilation gap between the insulation and the boards, as the Energy Saving Trust advises. Never compress insulation under boards or boxes.

Fix plumbing leaks immediately. Pipes and water tanks in the loft are a common source of slow leaks that go unnoticed for months. Lag all loft pipes with pipe insulation and fit an insulating jacket to any cold water tank, because the loft gets colder once it is insulated. Check for drips every year.

Check annually in autumn. A 5-minute loft inspection each September, before you turn the heating on, catches problems early. Look for damp, pest damage, and displaced sections.

When to upgrade your loft insulation

Even if your insulation is in good condition, upgrading makes financial sense in these situations:

Before installing a heat pump. Better insulation means a smaller, cheaper heat pump and lower running costs for as long as you own it. This is particularly important for old houses moving to heat pumps.

Before selling your home. Loft insulation directly improves your EPC rating. Buyers see your EPC, and landlords must meet a minimum EPC rating to let a property.

If your energy bills are higher than expected. Use our average energy bills guide to compare your bills against typical UK households. If you are paying significantly more, inadequate insulation is often the cause.

Frequently asked questions

Does loft insulation degrade over time?

Not much, if it stays dry and uncompressed. The Energy Saving Trust gives correctly installed loft insulation a lifespan of around 40 years. What usually goes wrong is damage rather than age: boxes or boards squashing it, leaks soaking it, or pests pulling it apart. Loose fill materials can also settle and thin out. Use our insulation calculator to see what a top-up would save.

When should you replace loft insulation?

Replace loft insulation if it is wet, mouldy, badly compressed or damaged by pests, after fixing the cause. If mineral wool is dry, springy and 270mm deep, it does not need replacing whatever its age. Leave vermiculite undisturbed until it has been tested for asbestos. Our DIY loft insulation guide covers laying new rolls.

Can you put new loft insulation on top of old?

Yes, if the old layer is dry and in good condition. Lay new mineral wool rolls at right angles to the joists over the existing layer to reach 270mm in total, without compressing what is underneath. The Energy Saving Trust puts a professional top-up from 120mm at about £600.

How thick should loft insulation be in the UK?

At least 270mm of mineral wool, the depth building regulations expect in new homes. Many older lofts have much less. Topping up is worth doing, but the measured saving is modest: homes used a median 3.2% less gas after loft insulation, about £30 a year for a 3 bed detached house on gas.

Does old vermiculite loft insulation contain asbestos?

It can. Some old vermiculite loft insulation may contain asbestos, and the Health and Safety Executive says any home built or refurbished before 2000 may contain asbestos. You cannot tell by looking, so have loose granules tested by an accredited laboratory before disturbing them, and leave any removal to a specialist.
