Condensation and Mould in UK Homes: Causes, Fixes, and Prevention

The short answer

  • Three causes: cold surfaces, too much moisture and too little heating, so ventilate, heat and insulate together
  • Ventilate first, as it is cheapest and fastest, then insulate, then heat
  • At least 18°C in the rooms you use, as the NHS advises, and keep the home steadily warm

Condensation happens when warm, moist air meets cold surfaces like windows, external walls and corners. Left alone, it leads to mould, which government guidance says can harm anyone's health. The fix is not just ventilation or just insulation; it is a combination of ventilation, heating and insulation. This guide explains why condensation happens, which fixes actually work, and the order to do them in.

Why condensation happens

Condensation forms when water vapour in the air hits a surface that is below the dew point temperature. Cooking, showering, drying clothes and even breathing all put water into the air, and government guidance notes that this moisture builds up if it cannot be removed. It then settles on the coldest surfaces: single-glazed windows, uninsulated external walls, corners where two external walls meet, and behind furniture against cold walls.

Mould grows on surfaces that stay damp. Government guidance on damp and mould says damp and mould are a risk to anyone's health, and the risk is greatest for people with an existing health condition such as asthma or another lung condition, people with a weakened immune system, pregnant women, children and young people, older people, and people who are housebound or have limited mobility.

The three causes (and their fixes)

1. Cold surfaces (insulation problem)

The colder a surface, the more likely condensation will form on it. In winter an uninsulated external wall can be several degrees colder on the inside than the room air, so ordinary indoor humidity condenses on it. Government guidance notes that homes that are poorly insulated and heated are more prone to condensation on the inside of external walls and windows.

Fix: Insulate the cold surfaces. Cavity wall insulation raises the temperature of the inside of the wall, and the Energy Saving Trust lists a lower risk of condensation on walls among its benefits. Internal solid wall insulation does the same for older homes with solid walls. Double or triple glazing raises window surface temperature. Even draught-proofing helps by stopping cold air washing over internal surfaces.

2. Too much moisture (ventilation problem)

If moisture produced inside the home cannot escape, humidity builds up. Common sources include drying clothes indoors, cooking without an extractor fan, long showers without ventilation, and portable bottled gas heaters, which give off water vapour as they burn.

Fix: Ventilate. Use extractor fans in kitchens and bathrooms (and actually turn them on). Open trickle vents on windows. Do not block air bricks. Dry clothes outdoors or in a tumble dryer vented to the outside, not on a radiator indoors. If you use a portable bottled gas heater, switch to another form of heating if you can: it adds moisture and needs ventilation to burn safely.

3. Not enough heating (temperature problem)

Cold air holds less moisture than warm air. If you do not heat your home adequately, the air reaches saturation point with less moisture in it, and condensation forms more easily. This is why condensation is worst in unheated bedrooms with the door closed overnight.

Fix: The NHS advises heating the rooms you regularly use, such as the living room and bedroom, to at least 18°C. Keeping the home steadily warm, rather than letting rooms go cold for long spells, also helps prevent condensation. Thermostatic radiator valves let you keep less used rooms cooler without letting them go cold.

The right order

Fix ventilation first (cheapest and fastest). Then insulation (most impactful, may be free through ECO4 until 31 December 2026). Then heating. Do not insulate without addressing ventilation, as a tighter home traps more moisture. Do not just heat more without ventilating, as you are adding energy cost without solving the root cause.

Room by room solutions

Bathroom

Install or upgrade the extractor fan. It should run during showering and for a while afterwards. If you do not have one, consider a humidistat-controlled fan, which turns on automatically when humidity rises. Keep the bathroom door closed while showering to prevent moisture spreading. Wipe down cold surfaces (tiles, windows) after showering.

Kitchen

Always use the extractor fan when cooking. Cover pans with lids. If you do not have an extractor, open a window while cooking and keep the kitchen door closed. Avoid drying clothes on radiators in the kitchen.

Bedrooms

Keep trickle vents open, or open a window for a short while each morning to clear the moisture that builds up overnight from breathing. Pull furniture a little away from external walls to let air circulate behind it. Do not completely seal bedroom doors overnight if the room has no independent ventilation.

Whole house

A hygrometer shows how humid your rooms are. If humidity stays high day after day and windows stream every morning, you have a ventilation, heating or insulation problem. Rule out leaks and rising or penetrating damp before assuming all moisture is condensation.

When it is not condensation

Not all damp is condensation. Rising damp (moisture coming up through the floor and walls from the ground) and penetrating damp (rain getting through the walls or roof) require different fixes. If you see damp patches on ground-floor walls that are worse in wet weather, or damp that appears on internal walls that are not external-facing, it may be structural damp rather than condensation. A qualified damp surveyor can diagnose the cause.

If you rent, report damp and mould to your landlord in writing. Since 27 October 2025, Awaab's Law has required social landlords in England to investigate and fix dangerous damp and mould within set time limits.

Insulation and condensation

There is a common fear that insulating your home makes condensation worse. This is only true if you insulate without maintaining ventilation. Insulation raises internal surface temperatures, which directly reduces condensation risk. But if you seal a home tightly (with insulation and draught-proofing) and do not provide adequate ventilation, the moisture has nowhere to go.

The solution is simple: insulate and ventilate. Keep trickle vents open. Ensure extractor fans work. If doing a comprehensive retrofit, consider mechanical ventilation with heat recovery (MVHR), which provides fresh air while recovering much of the heat.

Frequently asked questions

What causes condensation in UK homes?

Moist air from cooking, washing, drying clothes and breathing meeting cold surfaces such as windows and poorly insulated walls. Too little ventilation lets the moisture build up, and too little heating leaves surfaces cold. Fix all three: ventilate, heat and insulate.

Will insulation make condensation worse?

Only if you seal the home without keeping it ventilated. Insulation raises the temperature of inside surfaces, which cuts condensation, but keep trickle vents open and extractor fans working, and never block air bricks or vents.

How do I stop mould coming back?

Remove the moisture at source: use extractor fans, dry clothes outside or in a vented dryer, keep trickle vents open, heat the rooms you use to at least 18°C, and insulate cold walls. If mould keeps coming back, look for a leak or other source of damp, and if you rent, tell your landlord in writing.
