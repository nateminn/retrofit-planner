Heat Pump for a Victorian Terrace: Cost and What It Takes in 2026

The short answer

  • About £12,200 to £12,700 installed and £4,700 to £5,200 after the £7,500 grant, with most jobs £10,500 to £15,500
  • 7 to 9 kW for a three bedroom terrace, depending on how well it is insulated
  • £760 a year on a heat pump tariff, £107 less than gas, but £1,010 on a standard tariff

An air source heat pump for a three bedroom Victorian terrace costs about £12,200 to £12,700 installed, the median installers reported under the Boiler Upgrade Scheme in 2025/26 for the 7 to 9 kW unit it needs, with most jobs costing £10,500 to £15,500. That is the whole job as installers recorded it for the grant, cylinder, labour and VAT included; some homes also need larger radiators. It is about what the same job costs on a 3-bed semi, because solid brick walls mean a similar size of heat pump for a smaller floor area, and the pipework from the yard to a cylinder upstairs is long. The £7,500 Boiler Upgrade Scheme grant brings it to £4,700 to £5,200. A solid wall terrace using 9,790 kWh of heat a year costs £760 a year to run on a heat pump tariff, £107 less than gas, but £1,010 on a standard tariff, which is £143 more than gas. This is the cost companion to our old house guide, for a terrace built between about 1850 and 1914 with nine-inch solid walls, sash windows, suspended timber floors, a rear outrigger and no side access.

The median heat pump installation funded by the Boiler Upgrade Scheme cost £12,908 in April to June 2026, with a quarter of jobs under £11,128 and a quarter over £15,627 (DESNZ Boiler Upgrade Scheme statistics, Table Q1.1A). A Victorian terrace sits close to that median rather than at the bottom of the range, because a small house can still be a hard one to heat: a nine-inch solid wall loses more than twice the heat of a filled cavity, the sashes leak air, the floor sits over a ventilated void, high ceilings add to the volume, and the outrigger adds two exposed walls and a cold bathroom at the far end of the pipe run.

Installed cost is the 2025/26 Boiler Upgrade Scheme median for a heat pump of that size. The middle half of real jobs runs from about 14% below the median to 21% above it.

Within each row, the pipe run and the cylinder set the price. A cylinder cupboard still in the outrigger bathroom, directly above the unit, is a short job; a cylinder in a front bedroom, 15 metres of pipe away, is not. A four bedroom terrace, an end terrace or a long pipe run sits nearer the top of the range. Use our heat pump calculator for an estimate at your own insulation level and fuel.

Two things take away the advantage the terrace's size should give it. Radiators: the rooms are tall and the front rooms sit behind the coldest wall, so at a low design temperature many radiators change, while a higher temperature model keeps more of them. Pipework: insulated primaries have to reach the cylinder upstairs, usually up the outside of the outrigger. Suspended timber floors are the one break the house gives you, because lifting boards to run new pipe is quick work where a concrete-floored semi needs it chased in.

A Victorian terrace shares two walls, so on paper it should be cheap to heat. What size heat pump, and insulate first in cost order

7 to 9 kW, depending on insulation. Start with the loft at about £750 and draught-proofing at around £250; internal wall insulation, around £12,000, takes the unit from 8 kW to 7 kW.

Read the detail

On our model a three bedroom Victorian terrace needs a 7 to 9 kW heat pump, and where you land depends on what you do to the fabric first. The size for your house comes from a room-by-room heat loss calculation at the design outdoor temperature for your location. The RdSAP 10 assessment method gives a nine-inch solid brick wall a default of 1.7 W/m²K, against 0.7 for a filled cavity (BRE), so the walls are a large share of the loss in a bare terrace. A quote without the calculation was sized by guesswork; our heat pump sizing guide explains how it is done. What comes off the annual demand comes off the peak load too, and in the Boiler Upgrade Scheme data each kilowatt less is worth roughly £250 to £650 off the median installed price. The measures below are in rough order of cost, with the Energy Saving Trust's typical prices and, where it exists, the saving measured in real homes.

  • Loft to 270mm: about £750 from nothing or £600 as a top-up from 120mm, fitted. The bare or thin loft in many terraces is the cheapest of the fixes (Energy Saving Trust, May 2026). Homes that had loft insulation fitted or topped up in 2023 used a median 3.2% less gas the following year (DESNZ NEED). See our loft insulation guide and DIY loft guide; on a qualifying benefit, ECO4 may pay for it, provided the work is finished before ECO4 closes on 31 December 2026; the Great British Insulation Scheme closed on 31 March 2026.
  • Draught-proof the sashes: around £250 for professional draught-proofing of a whole house's windows and doors. The Energy Saving Trust puts the saving from draught-proofing windows, floors and doors at around £55 a year, and from sealing an unused chimney at around £45 (May 2026). Brush pile in the sash channels and meeting rail and sealed floorboard and skirting gaps do most of it. Our draught-proofing guide has the sash method.
  • Insulate the suspended floor: £1,400 to £2,500 for a typical suspended floor. The Energy Saving Trust puts the saving at about £40 a year for a mid-terrace house (May 2026). Mineral wool between the joists from a cellar or crawl space is the least disruptive route; otherwise the boards come up, which pairs well with new pipework. See our underfloor insulation guide and keep the air bricks clear.
  • Double or secondary glazing: around £4,800 for A-rated windows on a semi. The Energy Saving Trust puts the saving from replacing single glazing at about £85 a year (May 2026), so do it last unless the sashes are rotten. Secondary glazing keeps the originals, which matters in a conservation area.
  • Then internal wall insulation on the coldest rooms: around £12,000 for a whole typical three bedroom semi, less for a smaller terrace or a few rooms. Homes that had solid wall insulation in 2023 used a median 17.3% less gas the following year, the largest measured saving of any single measure (Energy Saving Trust, June 2026; DESNZ NEED). Start with the front room, the front bedroom and the outrigger. On our model it takes a terrace from band D to band C, 8,820 kWh to 7,497 kWh, and from an 8 kW unit to 7 kW. Our solid wall insulation guide has the costs; done badly it traps damp in the brick, so read our condensation guide first.

Measured savings are lower than modelled ones, partly because many homes were under-heated before and take some of the gain as warmth, so treat them as what homes like yours typically saw rather than the most you could save. The worked example in this guide is the band E terrace at 9,790 kWh, an unimproved or lightly improved solid wall house, which needs a 9 kW unit on our model and is the terrace many readers own. Model yours with the insulation calculator.

Flow temperature: higher keeps the radiators, lower keeps the bills down

A high temperature model designed at 50 to 55°C keeps more existing radiators, cast iron included, and costs less to install. A 45°C design replaces more radiators but runs more efficiently: the Electrification of Heat trial found heat pump efficiency falls steadily as flow temperature rises. Insulated terrace: take the lower design temperature. Not insulating yet: take the higher one, keep the radiators and turn the flow down as the fabric improves. Get the design flow temperature and the expected seasonal performance in writing.

Cast iron radiators, cylinder and pipework

Cast iron radiators can stay if they are big enough, and many are. Expect a few to change; pipework is where terrace quotes vary most, with primaries running 10 to 20 metres to the cylinder.

Read the detail

A heat pump runs at a lower water temperature than a boiler, so radiators need more surface to give out the same heat, and the lower the design flow temperature the more surface they need. Victorian terraces are better placed than most, because radiators fitted in the 1970s and 1980s were often generous for the walls and the rooms have wall space for tall column radiators. At a higher design temperature expect a few to change, usually the front room, front bedroom and outrigger bathroom, and more at a lower one; the work is part of the installed price rather than an extra. Our radiator sizing guide explains how installers decide.

Cast iron radiators can stay if they are big enough, and many are: they hold a lot of water and give off heat slowly, which suits a heat pump's steady low temperature. The installer works out the output at the design flow temperature from the section count and height, and if it falls short a second one in the same style is the usual answer. Where a radiator does change, a steel column radiator gives the period look for less than cast iron.

You also need a hot water cylinder sized to the household's hot water use; the Energy Saving Trust says it will usually fit a cupboard of at least 80 cm by 80 cm. Terraces that never had a combi often still have the cylinder cupboard in the outrigger bathroom, the best spot in the house, directly above the kitchen and one short run from the unit. Otherwise it goes in a bedroom cupboard, the alcove beside a chimney breast where a slimline cylinder fits, or the box room. Pipework is where terrace quotes vary most: the insulated primaries can run 10 to 20 metres, cheapest up the outside of the outrigger in a boxed duct, and narrow 8mm or 10mm microbore may need replacing, though Octopus says its Cosy heat pumps work with microbore of 10mm or larger. Ask the installer to lift a board and check pipe sizes at the survey.

Running costs: Victorian terrace

£760 a year on a heat pump tariff, £107 less than gas. On a standard tariff it costs £1,010, £143 more than gas; insulating the walls to band C cuts the tariff bill to £487.

Read the detail

These figures use the Ofgem price cap for 1 October to 31 December 2026: electricity 26.32p per kWh and gas 7.97p per kWh, a gas boiler at 90% efficiency, and a heat pump at the seasonal efficiency our model uses for each EPC band: 2.55 for the band E worked example, the lower quartile measured in the Electrification of Heat trial, rising to 2.78 at band D and 3.05 at band C. That is a cautious assumption for this house, because the same trial found pre-1919 homes did as well as newer ones. The Boiler Upgrade Scheme for a terrace

£7,500 off the quoted price, bringing the heat pump to £4,700 to £5,200. It is £9,000 until March 2027 for oil or LPG homes with no mains gas, and you no longer need a valid EPC.

Read the detail

The Boiler Upgrade Scheme pays £7,500 towards an air source or ground source heat pump in England and Wales. Until March 2027 it pays £9,000 where the home is heated by oil or LPG and has no mains gas connection (GOV.UK). Your MCS-certified installer applies, and since April 2026 the installer must take the grant off the price quoted to you, so the heat pump costs £4,700 to £5,200 at the medians. The scheme runs to 2030 and installations carry 0% VAT until March 2027. You need to own the house and be replacing fossil fuel or electric heating; since April 2026 you no longer need a valid EPC, and a bare loft has not blocked the grant since May 2024, though it should still be the first thing you fix. Read our BUS grant guide for the rules, our grant checker for anything else you qualify for, and our Warm Homes Plan guide for insulation funding. On a qualifying benefit, ECO4 can pay for wall insulation outright until it closes on 31 December 2026, and in England households on a low income can ask their council about the Warm Homes: Local Grant.

Is a heat pump worth it for a Victorian terrace

Only just, on a heat pump tariff: about £1,700 more than a gas boiler, paid back in about 16 years at £107 a year. On a standard tariff it never pays back, costing £143 a year more than gas.

Read the detail

Against a new gas boiler, about £3,500 for a three bedroom home according to DESNZ, including VAT, the band E terrace pays £5,200 for the heat pump after the grant, about £1,700 more. At £107 a year on a heat pump tariff that is a payback of about 16 years, inside the 20 years the government assumes a heat pump lasts (Boiler Upgrade Scheme impact assessment, 2022). That margin is thin: if 5% VAT returns to electricity in April 2027 the saving falls to about £69 a year and the payback runs past 20 years. A job at the top of the range, £8,000 after the grant, would not pay back within that life; one at the cheap end, £3,500 after the grant, costs no more than the boiler, and that is where a terrace with the cylinder cupboard still in the outrigger bathroom and radiators already generous tends to land. On a standard tariff the band E terrace does not pay back at all on our model, because the heat pump costs £143 a year more to run than the gas boiler. That is the honest position for this house on our cautious assumption. It is a comfort and carbon decision first, and a money decision on a heat pump tariff, or after the walls are insulated, which on our model puts the heat pump £17 a year below gas even on a standard tariff. Run your own figures with our boiler vs heat pump comparison, read heat pump vs new boiler for the 15-year view, and see why an electric boiler costs almost three times as much to run in our electric boiler vs heat pump guide.

Placement, conservation areas and two party walls

In the rear yard, usually on the side wall of the outrigger. It must pass the MCS 020(a) sound test at both neighbours' windows; a conservation area does not stop it, but a listed terrace needs planning permission.

Read the detail

With no side access the outdoor unit goes in the rear yard, and the usual spot is the side wall of the outrigger, facing across the yard: a short pipe run to the bathroom above, off the rear wall where the kitchen door and window are, and clear air in front. Avoid the corner where the outrigger meets the main house: under the MCS 020(a) method a unit against two walls and the ground counts as having three reflecting surfaces, which adds 3 dB to the result against one wall. A monobloc outdoor unit can weigh 100 kg or more, and it comes through the front door and out through the kitchen on a stair-climbing trolley; if your street has a rear alley, say so at the survey.

Permitted development in England used to require one metre from every boundary. That rule went in May 2025, and the conditions now are the MCS 020(a) sound calculation, which must show 37 dB or less at one metre from each neighbour's nearest habitable room window or door, a unit of no more than 1.5 cubic metres, only one heat pump on a house that is not detached, and nothing above ground floor level on a wall facing a highway (GPDO Class G; MCS). Wales moved to the same MCS 020(a) test on 1 June 2026 (MCS). With two neighbours whose rear windows are only a few metres away, the calculation has to pass on both sides. At one metre the Grant Aerona 290 range runs from 40 to 46 dB(A) and the Ecodan Ultra Quiet 8.5 kW is 45 dB(A); most terraces pass with the unit in the middle of the outrigger wall, and one forced towards a fence may need a quieter unit or an acoustic enclosure. Tell both neighbours before the survey. Our planning permission guide covers the rules and our noise guide the numbers.

Conservation areas do not stop you: an air source heat pump is still permitted development in a conservation area in England as long as it is not on a wall or roof facing a highway and is no nearer the road than the house, and a rear yard meets both tests. A listed terrace always needs planning permission for a heat pump, because permitted development does not apply anywhere within the grounds of a listed building (Planning Portal), and usually listed building consent for the unit, the pipework and the holes through the wall. Check for an Article 4 direction on your street too.

Getting quotes: what to check

Get three quotes from MCS-certified installers and compare the design, not the price. Each should include a heat loss calculation using the real solid wall figure, the design flow temperature, a radiator schedule and a sound calculation.

Read the detail

Get three quotes from MCS-certified installers and compare them on the design rather than the price. Each quote should include:

  • A room-by-room heat loss calculation using the real solid brick wall figure, not a default cavity figure, with the design outdoor temperature stated.
  • The design flow temperature and the seasonal performance expected at it, in writing.
  • A radiator schedule listing every emitter, which stay, which change, and their new sizes.
  • The cylinder size and location, ideally the outrigger bathroom, and the route and length of the primary pipework.
  • An MCS 020(a) sound calculation for the exact spot on the outrigger wall with both neighbours' nearest windows and doors marked, and confirmation of conservation area, Article 4 or listed status.
  • The unit and installation warranties, and confirmation that the Boiler Upgrade Scheme grant is already taken off the quoted price.

Installation usually takes longer in a terrace than in a semi, because of the pipe run and the carry-through. Find installers through the MCS installer search, and read our old house guide for the listed building and period detail that applies to every pre-war home.

If the unit sits on a plinth against a wall you share, anti-vibration pads under its feet stop the hum reaching next door's bedroom.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a heat pump cost for a Victorian terrace?

About £12,200 to £12,700 installed for a three bedroom Victorian terrace, the 2025/26 Boiler Upgrade Scheme medians for the 7 to 9 kW heat pump it needs, with most jobs costing £10,500 to £15,500. That is the whole job as installers recorded it for the grant, cylinder, labour and VAT included; some homes also need larger radiators. After the £7,500 BUS grant you pay £4,700 to £5,200. An unimproved terrace using 9,790 kWh of heat costs £760 a year to run on a heat pump tariff, £107 less than gas, or £1,010 on a standard tariff, which is £143 more than gas.

Do I need to insulate a Victorian terrace before fitting a heat pump?

Not to qualify for the grant, but it helps. On our model, taking a terrace from EPC band E to band D, usually through the loft, draughts and glazing, cuts heat demand from 9,790 to 8,820 kWh and the heat pump from 9 kW to 8 kW, and wall insulation towards band C takes it to 7,497 kWh and 7 kW. Measured savings from single measures are more modest: homes that had loft insulation used a median 3.2% less gas and homes that had solid wall insulation 17.3% less. See our solid wall insulation guide.

What size heat pump does a Victorian terrace need?

About 7 to 9 kW for a three bedroom terrace on our model: 9 kW at EPC band E, 8 kW at band D and 7 kW at band C with the walls insulated. The installer's room-by-room heat loss calculation decides, and it must use the real solid brick wall figure rather than a cavity default. Try our heat pump calculator first.

Can I keep cast iron radiators with a heat pump?

Yes, if they are big enough for the room at the design flow temperature, and many are, because cast iron radiators were often generously sized. A higher temperature design keeps more of them. Where one falls short, add a second radiator in the same style or swap it for a column radiator that suits the period look. See our radiator sizing guide.

Where does the outdoor unit go on a terrace with no side access?

In the rear yard or garden, usually on the side wall of the outrigger facing across the yard, away from the corner and from both fences, with the hot water cylinder in the bathroom above. The unit is carried through the house on a stair-climbing trolley. In England the MCS 020(a) sound calculation must show 37 dB or less at the nearest windows and doors of both neighbours. See our noise guide.

Can I fit a heat pump in a conservation area?

Yes. In England an air source heat pump is still permitted development in a conservation area as long as it is not installed on a wall or roof facing a highway and is no nearer the road than the house itself, so a rear yard installation qualifies. A listed terrace always needs planning permission for a heat pump, because permitted development does not apply to listed buildings, and usually listed building consent too. See our planning permission guide.
