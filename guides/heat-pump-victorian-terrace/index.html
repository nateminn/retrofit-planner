Heat Pump for a Victorian Terrace: Cost and What It Takes in 2026

Some links on this page earn us a commission if you buy. It costs you nothing, and it never changes the numbers or which option this page recommends.

An air source heat pump for a two or three bedroom Victorian terrace costs £10,000 to £14,000 installed, about what the same job costs on a 1930s semi despite the terrace being the smaller house, because solid brick walls mean a bigger unit for the floor area, more radiators change, and the pipework from the yard to a cylinder upstairs is long and awkward. The £7,500 Boiler Upgrade Scheme grant brings the heat pump down to £2,500 to £6,500; the whole job with radiators and a cylinder is £13,000 to £18,000 before the grant and £5,500 to £10,500 after it. A solid wall terrace using 9,790 kWh of heat a year costs £678 a year to run on a heat pump tariff, £189 less than gas, but £991 on a standard tariff, which is £124 more than gas. This is the cost companion to our old house guide, for a terrace built between about 1850 and 1914 with nine-inch solid walls, sash windows, suspended timber floors, a rear outrigger and no side access.

What a Victorian terrace heat pump really costs

The median heat pump installation funded by the Boiler Upgrade Scheme cost £12,908 in April to June 2026, with a quarter of jobs under £11,128 and a quarter over £15,627, according to MCS. A Victorian terrace sits near the median rather than at the bottom of the range, because a small house can still be a hard one to heat: a nine-inch solid wall loses roughly twice the heat of a filled cavity, the sashes leak air, the floor sits over a ventilated void, high ceilings add a fifth to the volume, and the outrigger adds two exposed walls and a cold bathroom at the far end of the pipe run.

Two lines take away the advantage the terrace's size should give it. Radiators: a terrace has 7 to 9, the rooms are tall and the front rooms sit behind the coldest wall, so at a 45C design most of them change; a high temperature model designed at 55C cuts that to two to four. Suspended timber floors are the one break the house gives you, because lifting boards to run new pipe is a morning's work where a concrete-floored semi needs it chased in.

Victorian terrace versus semi versus modern terrace

A Victorian terrace shares two walls, so on paper it should be cheap to heat. In practice even an uninsulated one uses about as much heat as a 1930s semi with filled cavities, 9,790 kWh against 9,900, and costs about the same to fit a heat pump to, because the access is worse and the pipe run longer. The two party walls buy back what the solid brick, the sashes and the bare floor give away.

What size heat pump, and insulate first in cost order

A Victorian terrace needs an 8 to 12 kW heat pump, and where you land in that range depends on what you do to the fabric first. The size comes from a room-by-room heat loss calculation at the design outdoor temperature, around minus 3C in the south of England, and a solid brick wall at around 2.1 W/m²K, against 0.55 for a filled cavity, is close to half the loss in a bare terrace. A quote without the calculation was sized by guesswork. Do the fabric work in cost order: what comes off the annual demand comes off the peak load too, and every kilowatt band you drop is £500 to £1,000 off the installation.

  • Loft to 270mm: £200 to £600 DIY, £400 to £800 fitted. The bare or 50mm loft in many terraces is the biggest of the cheap fixes, worth around 300 to 400 kWh a year. See our loft insulation guide and DIY loft guide; on a qualifying benefit it may be free under the Great British Insulation Scheme.
  • Draught-proof the sashes: £80 to £150 per window professionally, £15 to £30 DIY. Brush pile in the sash channels and meeting rail, sealed floorboard and skirting gaps and a balloon in any unused chimney take around 250 kWh off a heat loss that is often a quarter air leakage. Our draught-proofing guide has the sash method.
  • Insulate the suspended floor: £500 to £1,000 from below, £800 to £1,500 by lifting boards. Mineral wool between the joists from a cellar or crawl space is the least disruptive job here; otherwise the boards come up, which pairs well with new pipework. Worth 150 to 250 kWh. See our underfloor insulation guide and keep the air bricks clear.
  • Double or secondary glazing: £1,000 to £3,000 for secondary, £4,000 to £8,000 for replacement sashes. The dearest of the four, worth 200 to 300 kWh, so do it last unless the sashes are rotten. Secondary glazing keeps the originals, which conservation officers prefer.
  • Then internal wall insulation on the coldest rooms: £1,500 to £3,000 for two rooms, £4,000 to £7,000 for the whole house. Start with the front room, the front bedroom and the outrigger. This takes a terrace from 8,820 kWh to 7,497 kWh and from a 9 to 10 kW unit to 8 to 9 kW, so the walls save more heat on their own than the four cheaper measures put together. Our solid wall insulation guide has the costs; done badly it traps damp in the brick, so read our condensation guide first.

The worked example in this guide has done the first two steps and stopped: 270mm in the loft and draught-proofed sashes, a bare floor and single glazing. It uses 9,790 kWh a year, barely less than a bare terrace because the solid walls, the floor and the single glazing still dominate the loss, needs a 10 kW unit, and is the terrace most readers own. Flow temperature: 55C keeps the radiators, 45C keeps the bills down

A high temperature model designed at 50 to 55C keeps most existing radiators, cast iron included, and costs less to install. A 45C design replaces most radiators but runs more efficiently, because every degree of flow temperature is worth roughly 2 to 3% on the bill. Insulated terrace: take 45C. Not insulating yet: take 55C, keep the radiators and turn the flow down as the fabric improves. Get the design flow temperature and expected SCOP in writing.

Cast iron radiators, cylinder and pipework

A heat pump designed at 55C needs about 30% more radiator surface than a boiler at 70C, and at 45C about double. Victorian terraces are better placed than most, because the radiators fitted in the 1970s and 1980s were often oversized for the walls and the rooms have wall space for tall column radiators. Of the usual 7 to 9, expect two to four to change at 55C, usually the front room, front bedroom and outrigger bathroom, and five to seven at 45C, for £1,500 to £4,000. Our radiator sizing guide explains how installers decide.

Cast iron radiators can stay if they are big enough, and many are: they hold a lot of water and give off heat slowly, which suits a heat pump's steady low temperature. The installer works out the output at the design flow temperature from the section count and height, and if it falls short a second one in the same style is the usual answer. Where a radiator does change, steel column radiators give the period look at a third of the price of cast iron.

You also need a hot water cylinder, 150 to 210 litres. Terraces that never had a combi often still have the cylinder cupboard in the outrigger bathroom, the best spot in the house, directly above the kitchen and one short run from the unit. Otherwise it goes in a bedroom cupboard, the alcove beside a chimney breast where a slimline 450mm cylinder fits, or the box room, at £800 to £1,500 plus £300 to £600 for a cupboard. Pipework is where terrace quotes vary most: the 28mm insulated primaries run 10 to 20 metres, cheapest up the outside of the outrigger in a boxed duct, and any 8mm or 10mm microbore needs replacing. Allow £500 to £1,500, and ask the installer to lift a board and check pipe sizes at the survey.

These figures use the Ofgem price cap for 1 October to 31 December 2026: electricity 26.32p per kWh and gas 7.97p per kWh, a gas boiler at 90% efficiency and a heat pump at a seasonal COP of 2.6, the figure to expect from a solid wall terrace on a 50 to 55C design. As the house stands, the £189 saving is £3,780 over 20 years, plus the gas standing charge if you disconnect. The Boiler Upgrade Scheme for a terrace

The Boiler Upgrade Scheme pays £7,500 towards an air source or ground source heat pump in England and Wales, and since July 2026 £9,000 where an oil or LPG boiler is replaced. Your MCS-certified installer applies and the grant comes off the invoice, so the heat pump itself costs £2,500 to £6,500. The scheme runs to 2030, installations carry 0% VAT until March 2027, and a bare loft no longer blocks the grant, though it should still be the first thing you fix. Read our BUS grant guide for the rules, our grant checker for anything else you qualify for, and our Warm Homes Plan guide for insulation funding. On a qualifying benefit, ECO4 can pay for wall insulation outright.

Is a heat pump worth it for a Victorian terrace

Against a new gas boiler at £2,000 to £3,500, a heat pump for a terrace costs £2,000 to £8,500 more once radiators and the cylinder are included and the grant is taken off. At £189 a year on a heat pump tariff that is a payback of 11 to 45 years against a unit life of 20 to 25 years, so only a job at the cheap end of that spread pays for itself inside the life of the equipment: the cylinder cupboard still in the outrigger bathroom, radiators already oversized, and a boiler due for replacement anyway. On a standard tariff a solid wall terrace does not pay back at all, because at a COP of 2.6 the heat pump costs £124 a year more to run than the gas boiler. That is the honest position for this house. It is a comfort and carbon decision first, and a money decision only on a heat pump tariff, or after the walls are insulated, which lifts the COP to around 3.2 and puts the heat pump £47 a year below gas even on a standard tariff. Run your own figures with our boiler vs heat pump comparison, read heat pump vs new boiler for the 15-year view, and see why an electric boiler costs about three times as much to run in our electric boiler vs heat pump guide.

Placement, conservation areas and two party walls

With no side access the outdoor unit goes in the rear yard, and the usual spot is the side wall of the outrigger, facing across the yard: a short pipe run to the bathroom above, off the rear wall where the kitchen door and window are, and clear air in front. Avoid the corner where the outrigger meets the main house, because two walls reflect the sound and add around 3 dB to the noise calculation. A 10 kW monobloc weighs 100 to 180 kg and comes through the front door and out through the kitchen on a stair-climbing trolley; if your street has a rear alley, say so at the survey.

Permitted development in England used to require one metre from every boundary. That rule went in May 2025, and the conditions now are the MCS 020 noise limit of 42 dB at the nearest neighbour's habitable room window, a unit under 1.5 cubic metres, and nothing on a wall facing a highway. With two neighbours whose rear windows are two to four metres away, the assessment has to pass on both sides. Modern units are 45 to 55 dB at one metre and most terraces pass with the unit in the middle of the outrigger wall; one forced towards a fence may need an acoustic enclosure at £300 to £800. Tell both neighbours before the survey. Our planning permission guide covers the rules and our noise guide the numbers.

Conservation areas do not stop you: an air source heat pump is still permitted development in a conservation area in England as long as it is not on a wall or roof facing a highway and is no nearer the road than the house, and a rear yard meets both tests. A listed terrace needs listed building consent for the unit, the pipework and the holes through the wall. Check for an Article 4 direction on your street too.

Getting quotes: what to check

Get three quotes from MCS-certified installers and compare them on the design rather than the price. Each quote should include:

  • A room-by-room heat loss calculation using solid brick at around 2.1 W/m²K, not a default cavity figure, with the design outdoor temperature stated.
  • The design flow temperature, 55C or 45C, and the SCOP expected at it, in writing.
  • A radiator schedule listing all 7 to 9 emitters, which stay, which change, and their new sizes.
  • The cylinder size and location, ideally the outrigger bathroom, and the route and length of the primary pipework.
  • An MCS 020 noise assessment for the exact spot on the outrigger wall with both neighbours' nearest windows marked, and confirmation of conservation area, Article 4 or listed status.
  • The unit warranty, usually 5 to 7 years, the installation warranty, and confirmation that the installer will claim the Boiler Upgrade Scheme off the invoice.

Installation takes 3 to 4 days in a terrace, a day more than a semi for the pipe run and the carry-through. Find installers through the MCS installer search, and read our old house guide for the listed building and period detail that applies to every pre-war home.

If the unit sits on a plinth against a wall you share, anti-vibration pads under its feet stop the hum reaching next door's bedroom.

Frequently asked questions

How much does a heat pump cost for a Victorian terrace?

£10,000 to £14,000 installed. After the £7,500 BUS grant you pay £2,500 to £6,500 for the heat pump, or £5,500 to £10,500 with radiators and a cylinder. Running it costs £678 a year on a heat pump tariff at 9,790 kWh, £189 less than gas, or £991 on a standard tariff, which is £124 more than gas.

Do I need to insulate a Victorian terrace before fitting a heat pump?

Not for the grant, but you should. Loft, draught-proofed sashes and an insulated floor cost £1,500 to £4,000 together and take the heat pump from 12 kW to 9 or 10 kW, saving around £1,000 to install and £85 to £130 a year to run. Then insulate the coldest walls; see our solid wall insulation guide.

What size heat pump does a Victorian terrace need?

8 to 12 kW. Uninsulated needs 11 to 12 kW, one with loft, floor, draught-proofing and double glazing needs 9 to 10 kW, and one with internal wall insulation as well needs 8 to 9 kW. The heat loss calculation decides. Try our heat pump calculator first.

Can I keep cast iron radiators with a heat pump?

Yes, if they are big enough for the room at the design flow temperature, and many are. A 55C design with a high temperature model keeps most of them. Where one falls short, add a second in the same style or swap to a column radiator. See our radiator sizing guide.

Where does the outdoor unit go on a terrace with no side access?

In the rear yard, usually on the side wall of the outrigger, away from the corner and from both fences, with the cylinder in the bathroom above. The unit is carried through the house on a stair-climbing trolley. Both neighbours' nearest windows must pass the 42 dB noise test. See our noise guide.

Can I fit a heat pump in a conservation area?

Yes. In England it is permitted development in a conservation area as long as the unit is not on a wall facing a highway, so a rear yard is fine. A listed terrace needs listed building consent. See our planning permission guide.
