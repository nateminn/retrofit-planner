Underfloor Insulation Cost in the UK: Prices, Savings, and Options

The short answer

  • About £4,700 for a typical suspended timber floor job; doing it yourself from a cellar or crawl space costs only the materials
  • About £70 a year saved in an average house, or up to about £120 in a detached house
  • Over 50 years to pay back at full price, so do it when the floor comes up anyway, after loft and walls
CostsYour floor typeTimber floorsSolid floorsGrantsFAQs

Insulating a suspended timber floor costs about £4,700 for a typical installation, according to the Energy Saving Trust, and saves about £70 a year for an average house, or up to about £120 for a detached house. Solid floors can cost considerably more. At full price that is a very long payback, so floor insulation makes most sense when you are lifting the floor anyway, can reach it from a cellar or crawl space, or want to fix a cold, draughty floor for comfort. It usually comes after loft and wall insulation. The cost and approach depend on your floor type: suspended timber or solid concrete. This guide covers both, with DIY options, damp risks, and when the ECO4 scheme can fund the work.

ECO4 closes on 31 December 2026. Work under the scheme has to be done before then, so if you think you qualify, start now. The government has confirmed there will be no successor supplier obligation; instead it has committed £1.5 billion of extra grant funding for households on low incomes. Check what you qualify for.

What type of floor do you have?

Suspended timber floor: Common in homes built between the 1850s and the 1950s, including most Victorian and Edwardian houses. The floor sits on timber joists with an air gap underneath. You can usually identify it by the slight bounce when you walk, visible air bricks in the external wall near ground level, and floorboards visible at the edges of rooms. If you have a cellar or crawl space, you can see the underside of the floor.

Solid concrete floor: Usual in newer homes. The floor is a concrete slab sitting directly on the ground. It feels rigid with no give. There are no air bricks at ground level on the relevant walls. A home built to recent building regulations will usually have floor insulation already.

If you are unsure, check your EPC certificate, which states the floor type and insulation status. Use our EPC improvement planner to see how floor insulation would affect your rating.

Insulating a suspended timber floor

From below (best approach if accessible)

If you have a cellar or a crawl space deep enough to work in safely beneath the floor, insulating from below is the least disruptive method. Rigid insulation boards (PIR or EPS) are cut to fit between the joists and held in place with friction, battens, or netting. The void below must stay ventilated.

Doing it yourself costs only the materials. Check the joists first: the Energy Saving Trust says only go ahead if they are sound and free of damp, wet rot or dry rot, and get professional advice if they are not. Keep the space under the floor ventilated, and consider a vapour-permeable membrane, which manages moisture without trapping it against the timber. Avoid boxing the insulation in with plasterboard unless the joists are dry and the airflow is good, as it can trap moisture.

From above (when no access below)

If there is no access from below, the floorboards must be lifted, insulation fitted between the joists, and the boards relaid. This is more disruptive, and the Energy Saving Trust puts a typical professional suspended floor job at about £4,700, because it involves moving furniture, potentially relaying floor coverings, and careful handling of old floorboards that may crack during removal.

This approach is best combined with other floor work such as replacing damaged boards, sanding the floor, or fitting new carpets. Doing the insulation at the same time adds relatively little to the total disruption and cost.

Combine draught-proofing with floor insulation

If you are lifting floorboards to insulate, it is the perfect time to draught-proof the floor as well. Seal gaps between boards, around the edges, and along the skirting. This adds very little cost and addresses the other major source of floor-related heat loss.

Insulating a solid concrete floor

Solid floor insulation involves laying rigid insulation boards on top of the existing concrete, then a new floor surface on top. This raises the floor level, which affects door clearances, skirting boards, stair transitions, and kitchen units. If you replace 50% or more of a floor, building regulations require you to insulate it; the Energy Saving Trust gives the typical requirement as at least 70mm of high-performance rigid foam or around 150mm of mineral wool, depending on the floor.

The Energy Saving Trust says solid floor insulation can cost considerably more than a suspended floor, and this complexity is why. The work is disruptive: the room must be completely emptied, the old floor covering removed, insulation laid with a damp-proof membrane underneath, and a new floor surface (screed, floating boards, or tiles) installed on top.

Solid floor insulation rarely makes financial sense as a standalone project. It is best done when you are already renovating the ground floor, replacing the floor covering, or converting a ground floor room, when the extra cost of the insulation is a small part of work that is happening anyway.

Underfloor ventilation: do not block it

Suspended timber floors rely on air circulation underneath to prevent moisture buildup and timber rot. Air bricks in the external wall allow air to flow through the void beneath the floor. Never block these air bricks when insulating. The insulation should sit between the joists, not across the bottom of the void.

If air bricks are blocked by soil, debris, or overgrown plants, clear them. Blocked air bricks can lead to damp and rot in the floor timbers. Replace any air brick covers that are damaged.

Check for asbestos in old floor tiles

The Health and Safety Executive lists floor tiles among the materials that may contain asbestos in homes built or refurbished before 2000. If you need to lift or disturb old floor tiles to insulate a solid floor, have them tested before proceeding. Do not sand, break, or scrape them.

Grants and funding

The ECO4 scheme funds underfloor insulation for eligible households as part of a whole-house retrofit. If you qualify for ECO4, the assessor may include floor insulation in your package if your property needs it. ECO4 closes on 31 December 2026, and the work has to be finished by then. Use our grant eligibility checker.

If you pay yourself, installing floor insulation in a home is zero rated for VAT until 31 March 2027, and 5% after that (HMRC VAT Notice 708/6).

Where floor insulation fits in the priority order

Floor insulation saves less per pound spent than loft insulation (about £600 to £750) or cavity wall insulation (about £2,700, with a measured saving of about £105 a year for a 3 bed semi on gas). The recommended upgrade sequence is loft first, then walls, then draught-proofing, then floors. Floor insulation is the finishing touch, not the starting point.

However, if your suspended timber floor is the main source of draughts and cold feet, addressing it earlier delivers an outsized comfort improvement even if the pure financial return is lower.

Frequently asked questions

How much does underfloor insulation cost?

The Energy Saving Trust puts a typical suspended timber floor installation at about £4,700. Solid floor insulation can cost considerably more. Doing it yourself from a cellar or crawl space costs only the materials. ECO4 may fund the work for eligible households until 31 December 2026.

Is underfloor insulation worth it?

For comfort, often; on the bill alone at full price, rarely. The Energy Saving Trust puts the saving at about £70 a year for an average house and up to about £120 for a detached house, so a £4,700 job takes more than 50 years to pay back. It is worth doing when you can fit it yourself from below, or when you are lifting or replacing the floor anyway. Do loft and walls first.

Can I insulate under my floor myself?

If your suspended timber floor has accessible space underneath, such as a cellar or crawl space, you can fit insulation between the joists from below. Check first that the joists are sound and free of damp or rot, and keep the void under the floor ventilated. If there is no access from below, the floorboards have to come up, which is more disruptive.

What type of floor do I have?

If your ground floor sounds hollow or has a little give, and there are air bricks in the outside wall below floor level, you probably have a suspended timber floor, common in homes built between the 1850s and the 1950s. If the floor feels solid with no give, it is probably concrete. Your EPC also records the floor type.

Does underfloor insulation cause damp?

Not if it is done correctly. Keep the air bricks under a suspended timber floor clear so the void stays ventilated, only insulate over sound, dry joists, and use a vapour-permeable membrane where needed so moisture is not trapped against the timber. Solid floors usually need a damp-proof membrane under the insulation.
